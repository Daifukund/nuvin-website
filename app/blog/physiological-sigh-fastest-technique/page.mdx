# Physiological Sigh: A Stanford-Studied Way to Calm Down Fast

When anxiety is rising, you do not need a perfect breathing session. Try one slow **physiological sigh** now:

1. Breathe in gently through your nose.
2. Before breathing out, take one more short, quiet inhale through your nose.
3. Let the air leave slowly and comfortably through your mouth.
4. Return to an easy, normal breath.

That is one repetition. Try **one to three** repetitions, without forcing the size of either inhale or exhale.

The important part is the shape: a full inhale, a small top-up inhale, then a longer, unhurried exhale. It is also called **double inhale breathing** or **cyclic sighing** when repeated as a practice.

## What is the physiological sigh?

A physiological sigh is a natural breathing pattern that people often do without thinking, especially after crying, stress, or a long period of shallow breathing. It has three parts:

- a normal inhale
- a second, smaller inhale layered on top
- a longer exhale

For a deliberate version, keep it gentle. The second inhale is not a big gasp. Think of it as a small sip of air that completes the first breath.

You can do it sitting, standing, or lying down. If you are in a meeting, on public transport, or around other people, make the exhale quiet. Nothing about the technique needs to look noticeable.

## How do you do a physiological sigh when you are anxious right now?

Start by dropping the goal of making anxiety disappear immediately. Your only job is to complete a few comfortable breaths.

### Step 1: Let your shoulders be where they are

Do not force yourself to relax. If you can, unclench your jaw or lower your shoulders by a tiny amount. If not, that is okay too.

### Step 2: Take a smooth first inhale

Breathe in through your nose at a normal, easy pace. Stop before you feel strained or overly full.

### Step 3: Add the small second inhale

At the top of that breath, take a brief second inhale through your nose. This is the “double inhale” part. It should be small, not sharp.

### Step 4: Exhale longer than you inhaled

Breathe out slowly through your mouth. You might purse your lips slightly, as if cooling hot tea. Let the exhale be longer than the inhale, but do not count if counting makes you more tense.

### Step 5: Pause for one ordinary breath

Take one or two normal breaths before deciding whether to repeat. Notice only what is true: perhaps your chest feels a little less tight, perhaps nothing has changed yet, or perhaps you simply completed the exercise.

Try up to two more sighs if they still feel comfortable.

A useful cue is: **in, little top-up, long out**.

## How many physiological sighs should you take?

For an anxious moment, begin with **one to three physiological sighs**. More is not automatically better.

Repeated, large breaths taken too quickly can make some people lightheaded, tingly, or more aware of their breathing. If that happens, stop the exercise and return to your usual breathing. You do not need to “finish” a set.

If you want to use cyclic sighing as a regular practice, use a gentle pace for a short, planned period rather than trying to take one oversized breath after another. The Stanford study discussed below examined a daily practice, not an unlimited number of sighs during a stressful moment.

For immediate use, a good endpoint is simple: stop after a few repetitions, then put your attention on the next small thing you need to do. Feel your feet on the floor. Read the next email subject line. Take a sip of water. Let the breathing exercise support your day rather than become another task to monitor.

## Why might the physiological sigh feel calming so quickly?

When anxiety is high, breathing can become shallow, fast, irregular, or overly monitored. The physiological sigh gives you one clear, structured pattern to follow. The longer exhale may feel like a reset because it slows the rhythm of the breath and shifts attention away from racing thoughts and toward a physical action you can complete.

There is also a basic respiratory purpose to the two-part inhale. A deeper breath can help reopen tiny air sacs in the lungs that may not fully inflate during shallower breathing. That does not mean you need to maximize the breath. Gentle and comfortable is enough.

The experience is not identical for everyone. Some people feel a noticeable settling after one sigh. Others may only notice that they have interrupted a spiral for a few seconds. Both are valid outcomes. You are not doing it wrong if anxiety does not vanish.

Avoid treating the physiological sigh as a test of whether you are safe or okay. Use it as a practical regulation skill, then return to the situation in front of you.

## Is the physiological sigh backed by research?

Yes, with important limits.

A randomized controlled trial led by Stanford researchers compared several brief daily breathing practices with mindfulness meditation. In the study, **cyclic sighing** was the breathing practice associated with greater improvement in positive mood and a larger reduction in resting respiratory rate than mindfulness meditation. The reported differences were statistically significant at *p* < 0.05.

The study was published by Balban and colleagues in 2023. It is a meaningful result because the practices were brief and compared directly. It supports cyclic sighing as a promising, efficient breathing practice for everyday stress.

But it does **not** prove that one physiological sigh is the fastest way to calm every person in every setting. The study looked at a repeated daily practice over time, not a guaranteed instant outcome during a panic episode, medical emergency, or every kind of anxiety. Breathing techniques are self-help tools, not a diagnosis or a replacement for mental health care.

A fair takeaway is this: among brief practices studied in that trial, cyclic sighing performed especially well minute for minute. That makes it a sensible technique to try when you want one simple action now.

**Research source:** Balban MY, Neri E, Kogon MM, et al. *Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal.* Cell Reports Medicine. 2023;4(1):100895. doi:10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895.

## What should you do if the sigh makes you feel worse?

Stop and return to natural breathing. Try not to chase a deeper breath or repeatedly check how you feel.

You may prefer a more evenly paced practice, such as [box breathing](/blog/box-breathing-science), if the double inhale feels uncomfortable. You can also compare the patterns in <span data-internal="478-vs-box-vs-sigh">4-7-8 breathing, box breathing, and the physiological sigh</span>.

Do not practice deliberate breathing while driving, swimming, climbing, or doing anything that requires your full attention. If new or concerning breathing symptoms, chest pain, fainting, or other physical symptoms are present, seek appropriate medical care rather than trying to breathe through them.

If anxiety is frequent, getting in the way of daily life, or feels hard to manage alone, [learn when it may help to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional).

## When can you use this technique again?

Use the physiological sigh before a stressful conversation, while waiting for an appointment, after reading an upsetting message, or when you notice yourself holding your breath at your desk.

Keep the dose small: one to three gentle repetitions, then normal breathing. That is enough to make the technique easy to remember when you most need it.
