# Sunday Scaries: A Sunday Evening Routine for Monday Anxiety

If Sunday evening is starting to feel tight, heavy, or restless, try this first: put both feet on the floor and take one ordinary breath. You do not have to solve Monday tonight.

The Sunday scaries are a common name for anticipatory work anxiety, the dread that can build as the weekend ends. The goal is not to force yourself to love Monday or eliminate every anxious thought. It is to give your mind a small, clear container for what is ahead, then practice returning to the evening you still have.

## What is the Sunday scaries routine?

Use this 20-minute routine once this evening. Set a timer if that helps. Keep a piece of paper nearby, and do it away from your work inbox if possible.

This approach borrows from cognitive behavioral therapy, or CBT, and acceptance and commitment therapy, or ACT. Both approaches can help people notice unhelpful thought patterns and respond more flexibly. A short routine will not fix a difficult job, an overloaded schedule, or ongoing anxiety on its own. It can, however, interrupt the spiral of trying to mentally rehearse every possible Monday problem.

### Minute 1 to 3: Name the pattern

Say, quietly or on paper:

> “I am having anticipatory anxiety about work. My mind is trying to prepare me for Monday.”

This wording matters. Instead of treating every anxious prediction as a fact, you are noticing it as a mental event.

You might notice thoughts like:

- “I will never catch up.”
- “My manager will be disappointed.”
- “I cannot handle that meeting.”
- “The whole week is going to be awful.”

Do not argue with each thought right now. Label it: *a prediction*, *a worst-case story*, or *a work worry*. Naming the pattern creates a little space between you and the thought.

Then add: “I can feel anxious and still take one useful step.”

### Minute 4 to 10: Make a very small Monday landing plan

Anxiety often demands a complete plan for the entire week. Give it something more realistic: a plan for the first part of Monday only.

Write down these three things:

1. **My first task:** Choose one concrete action that takes 5 to 15 minutes. For example, “Open my calendar and list today’s top three priorities.” Not “Get caught up.”
2. **My next support:** Identify what will make that first task easier. For example, “Make coffee, close chat for 20 minutes, and use my task list.”
3. **My first boundary:** Decide one limit that protects your attention. For example, “I will not check email before I have reviewed my calendar,” or “I will take five minutes after the 10 a.m. meeting before replying.”

Keep this plan deliberately small. You are making an entry point, not proving that you can control the week.

If there is a genuinely urgent loose end, write one next action that can happen Monday. If it truly must happen tonight, choose a defined, time-limited task, such as sending one message in 10 minutes. Avoid opening a vague, open-ended work session. Those sessions often create more activation without producing a useful plan.

When you are done, put the paper somewhere you will see it in the morning. Tell yourself: “Monday has a place to begin. I do not need to keep planning it tonight.”

### Minute 11 to 15: Let the feeling be there without feeding it

This is the acceptance part. You may still feel dread after making a plan. That does not mean the routine failed.

Try this for two minutes:

1. Sit with your back supported, or stand with both feet grounded.
2. Notice where the anxiety shows up in your body, such as a tight chest, warm face, clenched jaw, or unsettled stomach.
3. Describe the sensation plainly: “Tightness is here.” “My stomach feels fluttery.”
4. Breathe normally. If a slower exhale feels comfortable, let the out-breath be a little longer than the in-breath. Do not strain or force deep breaths.
5. Repeat: “This is anxiety about Monday. I can make room for it without solving it right now.”

The point is not to reassure yourself that everything will definitely go well. No one can promise that. The practice is to stop treating discomfort as an emergency that requires more checking, planning, or scrolling.

If your mind says, “But what if Monday really is bad?” try: “Maybe parts of it will be hard. I will deal with the next part when it arrives.”

That is a more grounded response than either catastrophic prediction or forced positivity.

### Minute 16 to 20: Return to one Sunday activity

Choose one small activity that signals your evening is not over. Make it specific and sensory, not productive.

Examples include taking a warm shower, making tea, folding laundry while listening to one song, sitting outside for five minutes, or watching one planned episode of something familiar.

Before you begin, say: “For the next 20 minutes, I am not preparing for Monday.” If a work thought returns, do not wrestle with it. Briefly acknowledge it, then return to what you can see, hear, touch, taste, or do in this moment.

This is not avoidance. You already gave Monday a short planning window. You are choosing not to donate the entire evening to an unsolvable rehearsal.

## Why does Sunday night anxiety feel so convincing?

Sunday night anxiety can grow from uncertainty, unfinished tasks, difficult workplace dynamics, financial pressure, a demanding schedule, or memories of previous hard Mondays. Sometimes the shift from unstructured weekend time to work obligations is enough to set off a stress response.

Your mind may try to reduce uncertainty by running scenarios over and over. Unfortunately, repetitive worry can feel like preparation while leaving you more tense and less rested.

A useful question is: **“Is this thought helping me take a specific action, or is it keeping me in rehearsal?”**

If it leads to a clear, limited action, add that action to your Monday landing plan. If not, label it as worry and return to your Sunday activity.

This distinction is central to CBT-style coping. You do not need to prove a fearful thought wrong. You only need to decide whether engaging with it is useful right now.

## What if you keep checking work or planning the week?

Make the boundary physical when you can.

Put your work laptop in a bag or another room. Turn off work notifications for the evening. If you need your phone for something else, move work apps off the first screen or use a focus mode.

Then expect an urge to check. An urge is uncomfortable, but it is not an instruction. Try waiting 10 minutes before acting on it. During that time, repeat the phrase: “I already made my next-step plan.”

If you decide you genuinely need to check something, set a timer before you open it and define what you are looking for. When the timer ends, close it. The aim is not perfect discipline. It is reducing the automatic loop between anxiety and more work exposure.

## What if dreading Monday is keeping you awake?

Do the planning portion of this routine earlier in the evening, not in bed. Your bed is a difficult place to solve work problems, especially when you are already tired.

If worry is showing up at bedtime, keep the response simple: notice the thought, remind yourself that your Monday plan is written down, and return attention to your body or the room. For more guidance focused specifically on nighttime worry and sleep, read <span data-internal="sunday-night-insomnia-anxiety">Sunday night anxiety and insomnia</span>.

Avoid turning sleep into another task you must achieve. Resting quietly is still different from actively planning, checking, and rehearsing.

## When is it time to get more support?

Sunday scaries can be a temporary response to a busy or uncertain period. But you deserve more support if work dread is frequent, intense, affecting your sleep or daily life, leading you to avoid important responsibilities, or making it hard to function through the week.

A licensed mental health professional can help you understand the patterns involved and build support that fits your situation. You can also speak with a healthcare professional if anxiety symptoms are new, severe, or concerning. Read more about [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional).

For a difficult work event, it may help to practice the same pattern of naming the worry, making one plan, and returning to the present. Here is a guide for [calming down before a presentation](/blog/calm-down-before-a-presentation).

## What should you remember tonight?

You do not have to settle every work concern before bed. Try this sequence once: name the anticipatory anxiety, write a tiny Monday landing plan, allow the feeling without debating it, and return to one part of Sunday.
