# Burnout vs. Anxiety: How to Tell What You’re Feeling

If you are depleted, tense, dreading work, or unable to switch your thoughts off, it can be hard to know whether you are feeling burnout, anxiety, or both. You do not need to label it perfectly right now. Start by giving your nervous system a small pause, then look at the pattern with more clarity.

## What can you do when you feel overloaded right now?

Try this **feet-and-exhale reset** for about one minute. It will not tell you whether you have burnout or anxiety, and it is not a treatment. It can help you create enough space to notice what is happening without having to solve everything at once.

1. **Put both feet on the floor.** If you are standing, let your weight settle evenly through both feet. Notice the support under you: the floor, your shoes, the chair beneath you.

2. **Let your shoulders drop by one percent.** You do not have to force yourself to relax. Just unclench your jaw if you can, and allow your hands to rest.

3. **Take an ordinary, unforced inhale.** Do not try to take a very deep breath. A comfortable breath is enough.

4. **Let the exhale be a little longer.** For example, breathe in gently, then breathe out slowly until the breath naturally ends. Repeat four times. If counting helps, try in for three and out for four or five, without straining.

5. **Orient to three plain facts.** Silently name what is true in this moment: “My feet are on the floor.” “It is Tuesday afternoon.” “I am looking at a window.” Keep the facts neutral and concrete.

When you are stressed or worried, the body can shift toward a fight-or-flight state. That state is useful when action is needed, but it can also make every email, task, or thought feel urgent. A slightly longer, comfortable exhale and attention to your immediate surroundings may help interrupt that rush for a moment.

If breath focus makes you feel more uncomfortable, skip the counting. Keep your eyes open and simply press your feet into the floor while naming what you see around you.

After the reset, ask one useful question: **Is this feeling mostly tied to ongoing demands in one area of my life, especially work, or is it showing up across many situations?** Your answer is a clue, not a diagnosis.

## What is burnout?

Burnout is commonly used to describe a state of prolonged depletion related to chronic stress, most often in a work context. The World Health Organization describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon resulting from workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. Its description includes exhaustion, growing mental distance from or cynicism about one’s job, and reduced sense of effectiveness.

In day-to-day terms, burnout can sound like:

- “I have nothing left to give after work.”
- “I used to care, but now I feel detached or resentful.”
- “Even small tasks feel heavy, and I cannot get traction.”
- “A weekend helps a little, but I dread Monday before it arrives.”

The important feature is the pattern. Burnout often centers on a sustained mismatch between demands and resources. That may include workload, unclear expectations, low control, conflict, caregiving responsibilities layered onto work, or too little recovery time.

Burnout is not simply being tired after a hard week. It is usually a more persistent erosion of energy, engagement, and confidence. Still, only a qualified professional can assess what is contributing to ongoing symptoms. The word “burnout” should not be used to rule out anxiety, depression, sleep problems, medical concerns, or other causes of exhaustion.

## What is anxiety?

Anxiety is a common human response to perceived threat, uncertainty, pressure, or important stakes. It can involve worry, fear, restlessness, muscle tension, difficulty concentrating, irritability, stomach discomfort, or a sense that something needs to be handled immediately.

Unlike burnout, anxiety is not necessarily limited to work. It may show up before meetings, when checking messages, while trying to sleep, in social situations, around health or money concerns, or during quiet moments when your mind starts scanning for what could go wrong.

A person experiencing anxiety may still care deeply about work and want to perform well, but feel caught in overthinking, reassurance-seeking, avoidance, or a constant push to prepare for every possible problem. Work can absolutely trigger or intensify anxiety, which is why the experience of work burnout anxiety can feel so confusing.

## Is it burnout or anxiety if work is making you feel awful?

It may help to compare the center of gravity of the experience.

With **burnout**, the dominant feeling may be depletion: “I cannot keep giving at this pace.” You may feel emotionally flat, detached, ineffective, or deeply tired when you think about the job. The work itself, or the conditions around it, may feel like the main drain.

With **anxiety**, the dominant feeling may be alarm: “What if I cannot handle this?” You may feel keyed up, preoccupied with outcomes, afraid of mistakes, or unable to stop mentally rehearsing. The body may feel activated even when you are not actively working.

Those descriptions overlap. Someone who is exhausted by chronic workload can become increasingly worried about falling behind. Someone with persistent work-related anxiety can overwork, sleep poorly, and eventually become depleted. There is no clean line that you can reliably draw from a single bad day.

Instead of asking, “Which one is it, exactly?” try asking:

- What feeling comes first: drained and detached, or tense and alarmed?
- Does time away from work meaningfully change it?
- Do I feel this in other parts of life too?
- What has been happening with sleep, workload, support, and recovery?
- Has this pattern been getting stronger, lasting longer, or narrowing my life?

These questions can help you describe your experience more clearly to yourself, a trusted person, or a professional.

## Can you have burnout and anxiety at the same time?

Yes. They can reinforce each other.

Imagine a person facing months of unrealistic deadlines. At first, they may feel anxious before each workday and stay late to prevent mistakes. Over time, less sleep and little recovery can leave them exhausted. They may then feel detached, cynical, or unable to begin tasks, while also worrying intensely about their performance. That is a plausible overlap, not a personal failure.

The same cycle can happen outside a formal job. Long-term caregiving, school pressure, financial strain, and major life demands can create a similar mix of exhaustion and alarm.

Because the patterns overlap, avoid using one label to dismiss the other. “It is only burnout” can keep someone from discussing persistent worry. “It is only anxiety” can obscure an unsustainable workload or a workplace problem that needs practical change.

## Does waking up anxious mean you are burned out?

Not necessarily. Waking with dread, racing thoughts, or low energy can happen for many reasons, including stress, poor sleep, anxiety, depression, physical illness, medication effects, and workplace strain. It is not a self-test for burnout or an anxiety condition.

You may read about the cortisol awakening response, a normal rise in cortisol that often occurs in the first part of the morning after waking. Cortisol patterns vary considerably between people and days. They are influenced by sleep, timing, health, and many other factors, so they cannot tell you what you are feeling or provide a diagnosis. Expert guidance on measuring this response also emphasizes that it requires careful timing and interpretation in research settings ([Stalder et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2015.10.010)).

The practical question is simpler: **What happens next?** If dread repeatedly shapes your mornings, affects sleep, makes it hard to function, or is becoming harder to manage, that is worth taking seriously and discussing with a healthcare or mental health professional.

## What helps when the problem is burnout, anxiety, or both?

A nervous-system reset can make the next few minutes more manageable, but it cannot fix a chronic overload on its own. If burnout is part of the picture, support may involve practical changes as well: clearer boundaries, workload conversations, time away where possible, support from a manager or union representative, and realistic recovery time.

If anxiety is part of the picture, it can help to notice the loop between thoughts, body sensations, and behavior. For example, avoiding one difficult message may bring a brief sense of relief, but can make the message feel more threatening later. A professional can help you sort through these patterns and choose support that fits your situation.

You do not have to wait until you are completely unable to cope. Consider reaching out if these feelings persist, worsen, interfere with work or relationships, disrupt sleep, lead you to withdraw from things that matter, or make daily tasks hard to manage. A clinician can also help rule out physical contributors to fatigue, sleep changes, or anxiety-like symptoms.

Read more about signs that it may be time to seek support in [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional).

## What is the most useful takeaway?

Burnout often points to prolonged depletion and disconnection linked to chronic demands, especially at work. Anxiety often points to a state of alarm, worry, and threat-scanning that may extend across situations. But real life does not always separate them neatly.

For today, return to the smaller question: **What do I need in the next ten minutes, and what support might I need if this pattern keeps going?** A minute with your feet on the floor and a gentle longer exhale is not a complete answer. It can be a steady place to begin.
