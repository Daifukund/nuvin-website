# Panic Attack While Driving: Pull Over Safely and Calm Down

If panic rises while you are driving, your first job is not to force yourself to feel calm. It is to make the next few moments safer.

**Keep your attention on the road. Do not start a breathing exercise, search your phone, or try to analyze what is happening while the car is moving.** Reduce speed only as traffic conditions safely allow, signal, and pull over at the first safe, legal place you can reach.

Once you are parked, you can use one simple reset: a gentle, longer-exhale breathing practice. The goal is not to make every feeling disappear. It is to give your body a steadier rhythm while you decide what you need next.

## What should you do in the first minute of panic while driving?

Use this short safety script. You do not need to remember it perfectly.

1. **Keep both hands on the wheel and look ahead.** Put your attention on lane position, following distance, and the vehicles around you.
2. **Say one practical sentence:** “I am going to get to a safe place to stop.” This is not about arguing with anxious thoughts. It gives your mind one clear task.
3. **Signal and choose the safest available stopping place.** Depending on where you are, that may be a parking lot, rest area, gas station, exit, or a side street. Avoid abruptly stopping in an active lane.
4. **Slow gradually.** Do not brake sharply unless there is an immediate road hazard.
5. **Park fully.** Put the car in park, set the parking brake if appropriate, and give yourself a moment before doing anything else.

On a highway, a shoulder may be necessary in an emergency, but it can also be dangerous because traffic is close and fast. If your vehicle is still functioning and you can safely continue to an exit or other protected stopping place, that is often preferable. For planning ahead, see [highway driving anxiety](/blog/highway-driving-anxiety).

If you are too distressed to operate the vehicle safely, do not continue driving just to prove that you can. Call someone, arrange another ride, or wait until you are genuinely ready.

> **Safety caution:** Anxiety can overlap with symptoms of medical problems. If you have symptoms that feel severe, new, unusual for you, or medically concerning, such as chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath, confusion, or signs of a stroke, seek urgent medical help rather than trying to drive yourself. If you are in immediate danger, call local emergency services.

## How can you calm down once the car is safely parked?

Try **six rounds of gentle longer-exhale breathing**. This is one technique, not a test you have to pass.

Sit supported in the parked car. Let your shoulders drop if that feels comfortable. Keep your eyes open if closing them makes you feel less oriented.

### The six-round reset

1. **Notice the seat beneath you.** Feel your back against the seat and both feet where they rest. You are parked. Let that fact be your starting point.
2. **Inhale gently for about 3 seconds.** Breathe in through your nose if comfortable, or through lightly parted lips if your nose is blocked. Keep the breath small and easy rather than taking a big gulp of air.
3. **Exhale slowly for about 5 seconds.** Let the exhale be soft and unforced, as if you were cooling a spoonful of hot food without blowing hard.
4. **Pause normally, then repeat.** Do six rounds. If counting makes you more tense, skip the numbers and simply make each exhale a little longer than the inhale.

A useful phrase for the exhale is: “Slow out.” Keep it simple.

If you become more lightheaded, strained, or uncomfortable, stop counting and return to ordinary, natural breathing. Do not hold your breath or force very deep breaths. The practice should feel gentle.

You may notice your thoughts insisting that you need to leave immediately, solve the whole problem, or check every sensation. For these six breaths, you do not have to settle those questions. Your only job is the next easy exhale.

## Why use a longer exhale instead of trying to take deep breaths?

When people feel panicky, “take a deep breath” can sometimes lead to fast, effortful, or repeated large breaths. That may feel uncomfortable for some people. A smaller, slower breath with a slightly longer exhale is usually easier to do without turning breathing into another task to get right.

There is credible, but limited, research supporting brief structured breathing practices for stress and mood. In a 2023 randomized trial, Balban and colleagues compared several daily five-minute breathing practices over 28 days. The study found that structured respiration practices, particularly cyclic sighing, improved mood and reduced physiological arousal measures relative to a mindfulness meditation comparison. The study was not conducted during driving emergencies, and it does not show that breathing can make it safe to keep driving when you are impaired.

A 2023 meta-analysis by Fincham and colleagues pooled 12 randomized controlled trials involving 785 adults and found a small-to-moderate reduction in self-reported stress from breathwork interventions (standardized effect size g = -0.35). Breathwork studies vary widely in the techniques used, the people studied, and the outcomes measured. That means it is reasonable to use this as a practical calming tool, but not to treat it as a guaranteed fix for panic or a substitute for medical or mental health care.

References: Balban et al., *Cell Reports Medicine* (2023), DOI: 10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895. Fincham et al., *Scientific Reports* (2023), meta-analysis of 12 randomized controlled trials.

## What should you check before driving again?

After the six breaths, wait another minute or two. Then make a safety decision, not a bravery decision.

Ask yourself:

- Can I focus on the road, mirrors, signs, and other cars?
- Do I feel physically steady enough to operate the pedals and steering wheel?
- Can I make a simple plan for the next few minutes, such as driving to a nearby gas station or going home by familiar streets?
- Am I still distracted by intense sensations or urges that could pull my attention away from driving?

If your answer is no, stay parked. Text or call a trusted person, order a ride if available, or give yourself more time. If your answer is yes, make the restart deliberately small.

For example: drive only to the next safe destination, not necessarily all the way through your original plan. Choose slower, familiar roads when possible. Avoid immediately getting onto a busy highway if that is likely to overwhelm your attention.

Before moving, put your phone away and set navigation while parked, if you need it. Then return your full attention to driving. Do not use a guided audio exercise while you need to process traffic. If you use the [Nuvin app](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/nuvin-anxiety-relief/id6753338724), open it only after you have parked safely, never while the vehicle is moving.

## What if the panic feeling returns after you start driving?

Treat a return of strong anxiety as a cue to reassess safety, not as a command to push harder.

You might be able to continue for a short distance if you remain alert and in control, while calmly following the same plan to reach a safe place to stop. Keep your focus external and practical: the car ahead, your lane, the next turn, the next safe parking area.

If the feelings are escalating or your concentration is slipping, pull over again when it is safe. Repeating the safety plan is not failure. It is responsible driving.

Try not to make a rule such as, “I must never feel anxious in a car.” That goal can make every normal sensation feel like a warning. A more workable rule is: “If anxiety rises, I know how to get safe, pause, and choose my next step.”

For a parked-only plan focused on the anxiety surge itself, read [how to stop a panic attack](/blog/how-to-stop-a-panic-attack).

## Is it a good idea to avoid driving after one panic episode?

You may reasonably choose to take a break from driving for the day. After that, complete avoidance can sometimes make driving feel more threatening over time, especially if you begin avoiding more routes, bridges, highways, or being alone in the car.

The answer is not to force yourself into a long or difficult drive while overwhelmed. A gradual, planned return is often more useful. That might mean sitting in a parked car, taking a short route with a supportive passenger, or driving a familiar loop at a quieter time. The pace should be manageable and safe.

If driving anxiety keeps changing your routines, work, relationships, or ability to travel, professional help can be useful. Cognitive behavioral therapy and exposure-based approaches are commonly used for persistent phobias and panic-related avoidance. A qualified clinician can help you build a gradual plan tailored to your history, health, and driving situation. Learn more about [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional).

## How can you prepare before your next drive?

Preparation is not about trying to guarantee that anxiety will never show up. It is about reducing decisions you would otherwise have to make under stress.

Before leaving, choose one or two likely stopping places along your route, such as a grocery store parking lot, rest area, or gas station. Put the route into navigation before the car moves. Keep water, your charger, and any needed medications accessible, but do not use them as a reason to divide attention while driving.

Most importantly, rehearse the one plan from this article:

**Drive safely. Pull over safely. Park. Take six gentle breaths with a longer exhale. Decide whether you are ready to continue.**

You do not need perfect calm to make a safe choice. You need enough steadiness and attention for the next step. If that steadiness is not there yet, staying parked and getting support is the right next step.
