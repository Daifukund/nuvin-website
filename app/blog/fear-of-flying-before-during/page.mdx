# Fear of Flying: A Breathing Technique to Use Before and During Your Flight

If you are scared of flying, you do not need to solve every anxious thought before boarding. Start with one small, repeatable action: a gentle double inhale followed by a longer exhale.

You can do it in your seat, while waiting at the gate, or when the cabin gets bumpy. It is quiet, needs no equipment, and can be used offline in airplane mode.

## Can you try this breathing technique right now?

Sit or stand in a position that feels steady. Let your hands rest in your lap, on your bag, or on the armrests. Keep your eyes open if that helps you stay oriented to your surroundings.

Then repeat this cycle for about one to three minutes:

1. **Breathe in gently through your nose.** Make it a comfortable inhale, not your biggest possible breath.
2. **Take a second, smaller top-up inhale.** It is a brief sip of air, not a gulp.
3. **Breathe out slowly and softly.** Let the exhale be longer than both inhales together. You can breathe out through lightly pursed lips, as if cooling a spoonful of tea.
4. **Pause naturally, then begin again.** Do not hold your breath or force a count.

A useful rhythm might be a two-second first inhale, a one-second top-up inhale, and a five- to seven-second exhale. The exact timing is less important than keeping it light and letting the out-breath be unhurried.

Try six to 12 cycles. If counting makes you feel pressured, skip the numbers and focus on the shape of the breath: in, a small second in, then a long easy out.

If this feels uncomfortable, lightheaded, or makes your anxiety worse, stop and return to your usual breathing. You do not need to push through discomfort for the exercise to “work.”

## Why use a longer exhale when flight anxiety rises?

Fear of flying can make breathing feel shallow, fast, or effortful. You may notice yourself scanning for sounds, sensations, or signs that something is wrong. A brief breathing practice gives your attention one concrete job that you can do without leaving your seat.

The double-inhale, longer-exhale pattern is often called a cyclic sigh or physiological sigh. The goal is not to erase anxiety, guarantee calm, or make you like flying. It is to create a slower, steadier breathing pattern while you move through a difficult moment.

Keep the effort low. Very deep or rapid breathing can feel unpleasant, particularly when you are already anxious. Gentle is the point.

## How can you use it before your flight?

Use the same cycle at three predictable moments, rather than waiting until anxiety is at its peak.

**At the gate:** Do one minute while seated. Keep your bag and boarding details where you can see them. You are not trying to feel perfectly calm before you board. You are practicing returning to one manageable action.

**In your seat before the doors close:** Buckle your seat belt, place both feet on the floor if you can, and do another six cycles. Pair each exhale with a simple phrase such as, “This is the next breath,” or, “I can stay with this moment.”

**During takeoff:** If you tend to brace or hold your breath, begin the cycle before the plane starts moving. Continue at a comfortable pace. You can pause to listen to crew instructions, then resume when you are ready.

Repeating the technique before the hardest part of the flight helps you learn it when there is slightly less pressure. It also means you are not trying to remember a new method in the middle of a spike of flight anxiety.

## What can you do during turbulence?

Turbulence can be especially difficult when you are scared of flying because it adds movement, noise, and uncertainty. Start with practical safety steps first: remain seated when instructed, keep your seat belt fastened, and follow the crew’s directions.

Then make the breathing exercise smaller.

Instead of aiming for a long practice, do just three cycles:

- First inhale, gentle.
- Second inhale, small.
- One slow exhale.

On each exhale, deliberately soften one area that is bracing, such as your jaw, hands, or shoulders. You do not need to make the turbulence stop or convince yourself to feel different. Your job is simply to complete the next exhale.

When your mind jumps ahead, come back to a detail you can verify in the present: the feeling of the seat under you, your feet in your shoes, or the sensation of air leaving your lips. Then begin the next cycle.

If being in a confined public space is part of what makes the experience hard, this guide on [calming down in public when you can't leave](/blog/calm-down-in-public-when-you-cant-leave) may also help you prepare.

Set yourself up before boarding, while you still have service. Keep the plan deliberately short. A helpful note might say:

> Gentle inhale. Small top-up inhale. Long soft exhale. Repeat six times.

You might also choose one non-breathing anchor that is already available, such as holding a cool drink, looking at a fixed point in the cabin, or noticing the pressure of your back against the seat. This is not a second exercise to master. It is simply a place to put your attention between breath cycles.

If travel anxiety also shows up on the road, you may find some overlap with [highway driving anxiety](/blog/highway-driving-anxiety), particularly around anticipating a situation you cannot immediately leave.

## Do breathing exercises help fear of flying?

Breathing exercises have research support for reducing stress on average, but the evidence should be interpreted carefully. They are not a cure for flight phobia, and a breathing study does not tell us that one technique will work the same way for every person on every flight.

A Stanford randomized controlled trial by Balban and colleagues found that brief daily structured breathing practices, including cyclic sighing, improved mood and reduced physiological arousal measures over the study period. The study did not test people specifically during air travel, so it cannot prove that cyclic sighing will prevent flight anxiety on a plane. You can read the paper in *Cell Reports Medicine* (2023): [DOI: 10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895).

A 2023 meta-analysis by Fincham and colleagues in *Scientific Reports* pooled 12 randomized controlled trials involving 785 adults. It found a small-to-moderate average reduction in self-reported stress from breathwork, with an effect size of g = -0.35. That is encouraging, but it is not a reason to oversell breathwork. The studies varied in their methods, participants, and breathing practices, and stress is not the same thing as fear of flying.

The practical takeaway is modest: this is a low-key tool worth trying. Think of it as a way to support yourself through the next few minutes, not as a test of whether you can make anxiety disappear.

## When should you seek more support for a fear of flying?

Consider professional support if fear of flying leads you to avoid important trips, causes major distress for weeks before travel, or feels difficult to manage even with self-help strategies. Exposure-based therapy and cognitive behavioral approaches are commonly used for specific fears, and a qualified clinician can help create a paced plan that fits your situation.

You do not have to wait until a trip is imminent to ask for help. Read more about [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional), especially if this fear is shrinking your life or repeatedly disrupting work, family, or travel plans.
