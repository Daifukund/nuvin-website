# Anxiety Won’t Let You Sleep Tonight? Try This 15-Minute Offline Wind-Down

If anxiety will not let you sleep tonight, do not try to solve every thought in your head. Give your body a small, repeatable cue that the day is over instead.

This is one 15-minute wind-down sequence for the moment when you are too anxious to sleep. It uses a long-exhale breathing pattern, simple sensory grounding, and gentle muscle release. You do not need a screen, special equipment, or perfect calm before you start.

The goal is not to force sleep. It is to lower the struggle around sleep and make rest more possible.

## Can you set up the next 15 minutes first?

Put your phone face down and out of reach, or leave it in another room. Dim the light. If you are in bed and feel keyed up, sit on the edge of the bed or in a nearby chair with your feet supported.

Set a timer for 15 minutes if that feels helpful, then do not check the time again. Clock-watching can turn a hard night into a running calculation about how little sleep is left.

Use this sentence as your only job for the next few minutes:

> I do not have to make sleep happen. I only have to practice winding down.

If a thought says, “But I need to sleep now,” notice it, then come back to the next physical instruction. You can handle tomorrow later. For now, your task is just this sequence.

## How do you do the 15-minute wind-down?

### Minutes 1 to 5: Breathe out longer than you breathe in

Let your shoulders drop. Unclench your jaw if you can. Breathe in gently through your nose for about three seconds, then breathe out slowly for about five or six seconds.

Keep the inhale easy. The longer exhale is the important part.

Try this rhythm for five minutes:

1. Inhale softly, counting 1, 2, 3.
2. Exhale slowly, counting 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, or 6.
3. Pause naturally, without holding your breath on purpose.
4. Repeat.

You do not need to take huge breaths. Big breaths can feel unpleasant when you are already anxious. Think quiet, low-effort breathing, like you are gently fogging a window with your mouth closed.

If counting makes you more alert, skip the numbers. Simply let each exhale be a little longer than the inhale.

If you feel lightheaded, uncomfortable, or more tense while trying to control your breathing, stop the pattern and return to your natural breath. Comfort matters more than technique.

### Minutes 6 to 9: Orient to the room you are actually in

Anxiety can pull attention into tomorrow, old conversations, imagined disasters, or the question of why you are still awake. Grounding brings attention back to the immediate environment without asking you to argue with those thoughts.

Keep breathing gently and look around the room. Slowly name, silently or out loud:

- Five neutral things you can see, such as a curtain, a corner of the ceiling, or a book.
- Four things your body can feel, such as the sheet against your leg, your back against the chair, or cool air on your hands.
- Three sounds you can hear, including quiet sounds.

Stay descriptive rather than evaluative. Instead of “This room is a mess” or “I should be asleep,” try “dark blue blanket,” “low hum,” or “feet on the floor.”

If you lose your place because your mind races, that is not failure. Start again with one thing you can see. For more help with the thought spiral itself, try [how to quiet racing thoughts at night](/blog/quiet-racing-thoughts-at-night) on another occasion, not as a new task to complete right now.

### Minutes 10 to 13: Soften the places that brace for danger

Now use a gentle tension-and-release scan. This is not a workout and it should not hurt.

Start at your forehead. Briefly notice whether it is tight, then let it smooth without forcing it. Move down through your jaw, shoulders, hands, belly, thighs, and feet.

At each area, use this small cycle:

1. Notice the contact or tension.
2. On the next exhale, soften that area by about 5 percent.
3. Move on, even if it does not fully relax.

You might press your feet lightly into the floor for one breath, then release. You might squeeze your hands gently, then open them. The point is to give your body a clear contrast between bracing and easing, not to get rid of every sensation.

When anxious thoughts interrupt, use a brief label: “thinking,” “planning,” or “worrying.” Then return to the body part you were on.

### Minutes 14 to 15: Choose rest, then make one simple decision

For the last two minutes, stop trying to improve anything. Let your breath be natural. Say to yourself:

> Rest still counts, even before sleep arrives.

Then decide what comes next based on how awake you feel, not on what the clock says.

If you feel even a little drowsier, go to bed and let the routine end there. Keep the room dark and avoid picking up your phone for a “quick check.”

If you feel alert, frustrated, or like your bed has become a place to wrestle with anxiety, get up. Sit somewhere dim and comfortable and do something quiet and unstimulating, such as rereading a familiar few pages of a book. Return to bed when you feel sleepy again.

This is not a punishment for being awake. It is a way to avoid teaching your brain that bed is where you battle, scroll, or problem-solve.

## Should you stay in bed when anxiety is keeping you awake?

There is no need to leap out of bed the moment you notice anxiety. Start with the wind-down first.

But if you have been lying awake long enough that you are increasingly agitated, checking the time, or mentally rehearsing tomorrow, a brief reset out of bed can be more useful than staying and trying harder. Keep lights low, avoid work and social media, and return only when sleepiness starts to show up.

Do not turn this into another rigid rule. You do not need to calculate exactly how many minutes you have been awake. Notice whether the bed feels associated with rest or with struggle, then choose the gentler option.

## What if the thoughts return as soon as you lie down?

They may. A successful wind-down does not require an empty mind.

When a thought returns, avoid opening a debate with it. Try this three-part response:

1. Name it: “There is the work worry again.”
2. Postpone it: “I can consider this tomorrow.”
3. Redirect physically: feel the weight of your body on the mattress and lengthen one exhale.

Repeat as often as needed. Repetition is the practice.

## What does the research say about slower breathing for anxiety?

Breathing exercises are often oversold online, so it is worth being precise. They may help some people feel less stressed or more settled in the moment, but they are not a guaranteed sleep switch.

A randomized trial by Balban and colleagues, published in *Cell Reports Medicine* in 2023, compared brief daily structured breathing practices with mindfulness practice. The study found that cyclic sighing, a breathing pattern with an extended exhale, was associated with improved mood and lower respiratory rate. It studied daily practice, not a single bedtime routine, and it did not prove that the exercise treats insomnia or will make someone sleep on a particular night. DOI: [10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895).

A 2023 meta-analysis by Fincham and colleagues in *Scientific Reports* pooled 12 randomized controlled trials involving 785 adults. Slow-paced breathing interventions were associated with lower self-reported stress, with a modest average effect size of g = -0.35. The studies varied in their methods and participants, so this is useful support for a low-risk stress-management practice, not proof of a universal fix for nighttime anxiety.

That is why this routine is framed as an experiment for tonight. Try it gently. Notice whether it lowers the intensity of the struggle by even a small amount.

## When is nighttime anxiety more than an occasional hard night?

An occasional anxious night is common, especially during stress, change, illness, or a demanding week. Persistent sleep trouble deserves more support.

Chronic insomnia is generally described as trouble sleeping at least three nights a week for at least three months, with daytime effects. If that pattern sounds familiar, consider speaking with a qualified healthcare professional. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, often called CBT-I, is a well-supported treatment approach for chronic insomnia.

You do not have to wait until sleep becomes unbearable to ask for help. Read more about [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional), especially if anxiety or sleep problems are regularly affecting your days.

For tonight, return to the smallest version of the plan: one softer exhale, one thing you can feel, and one decision not to fight the whole night at once.
