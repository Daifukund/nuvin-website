# 60-Second Reset for Stress Between Meetings: A Shake-and-Stretch Routine

If one stressful task is ending and another is about to begin, try this 60-second physical reset before you open the next tab, answer the next message, or walk into the next meeting.

It is not about forcing yourself to feel calm. It is a brief movement break to loosen some of the physical tension you may be carrying and give your attention a clearer handoff.

## How do you do a 60-second reset for stress?

Set a 60-second timer. Stand if you comfortably can. If standing is not practical, use the seated version described below.

Keep the movements gentle. You do not need to shake hard, stretch deeply, or push through discomfort.

### Seconds 0 to 10: Plant your feet

Put both feet on the floor, about hip-width apart. Let your knees stay soft rather than locked.

Notice three physical points of contact: your feet in your shoes or on the floor, your hands, and your back or torso. You are not trying to empty your mind. You are simply orienting to where your body is right now.

Let your breath be natural. If a slower exhale feels comfortable, allow it, but do not force your breathing.

### Seconds 10 to 25: Loosen your hands and arms

Let your arms hang by your sides. Gently shake out your hands, then your forearms. Imagine you are flicking water from your fingertips, but keep it small and easy.

If it helps, alternate one arm and then the other. Keep your jaw unclenched if you notice it tightening.

The aim is not to perform a special kind of shaking. It is simply to interrupt the still, braced posture that can build up during a tense call, difficult email, or concentrated work.

### Seconds 25 to 40: Add your shoulders and upper body

Roll your shoulders up, back, and down once or twice. Then let your arms swing loosely at your sides for several seconds.

You can add a small side-to-side weight shift through your feet. Keep your head and neck comfortable, and make the range of motion smaller if you feel unsteady.

Notice whether any area feels particularly held, such as your shoulders, hands, forehead, chest, or stomach. You do not have to make that sensation disappear. Just let the movement be a chance to soften your grip a little.

### Seconds 40 to 55: Stretch one area that feels tight

Choose one simple stretch:

- Reach both arms overhead, then lower them slowly.
- Interlace your fingers and gently press your palms away from your chest.
- Place your hands on your hips and open your chest slightly.
- Turn your head gently to look over one shoulder, then the other.

Hold only to a comfortable point. Avoid bouncing, straining, or trying to achieve a big stretch in a short time.

### Seconds 55 to 60: Choose the next physical action

Stop moving. Feel your feet again. Then name one concrete next action: “Open the agenda,” “Get water,” “Write the first sentence,” or “Join the call.”

This last step matters. A reset is often more useful when it creates a bridge into the next task, rather than becoming another thing to do perfectly.

## What if you need to reset between meetings without leaving your chair?

Use a quieter seated version:

1. Put both feet flat on the floor.
2. Press your feet down gently for two seconds, then release.
3. Shake out your hands below desk level for 10 to 15 seconds.
4. Roll your shoulders, then reach your arms forward and round your upper back slightly.
5. Drop your shoulders and identify the first action for your next meeting.

This is especially useful when you have only a minute between video calls. You do not need privacy, workout clothes, or a major change of setting. The movement can be subtle enough to do at your desk.

## Why might a physical reset help after a stressful task?

Stress can show up physically. You might notice raised shoulders, a clenched jaw, shallow or held breathing, restless hands, or a rigid sitting position. A brief shake-and-stretch routine gives you a different action to take than staying frozen in the same posture while mentally replaying what just happened.

The goal is modest: create a moment of movement, notice tension, and transition to the next task. For some people, that may make it easier to refocus. For others, it may simply feel like a useful pause.

This is not a validated trauma treatment, and shaking is not a proven way to “release trauma.” It is better understood here as ordinary, gentle movement and tension-release. ## What does the research actually say about shaking and stress relief?

The evidence for shaking-based approaches, including methods sometimes grouped under tension and trauma releasing exercises, is limited and not strong enough to support broad treatment claims. That is why this routine should be viewed as a brief self-help movement practice, not therapy or a replacement for mental health care.

There is stronger, though still not perfect, evidence for related approaches. Research on progressive muscle relaxation, which involves deliberately tensing and releasing muscle groups, has found meaningful average reductions in anxiety symptoms in some studies. One meta-analytic estimate reported an effect around *d* = 0.57. Results vary across people, settings, and study quality, and that finding does not prove that a one-minute shake-and-stretch routine will have the same effect.

Research on a single bout of exercise suggests a smaller average effect on state anxiety. A 2015 meta-analysis by Ensari and colleagues estimated an effect around *g* = 0.16. That is a modest average finding, not a promise of immediate relief for every person.

Taken together, this evidence supports a reasonable, limited idea: gentle movement may be worth trying as a quick stress reset, especially when you are physically tense. It does not establish that shaking has a unique benefit or that it treats anxiety disorders.

## When should you make the reset smaller or skip it?

A physical reset should feel manageable, not like something to push through. Keep it seated or skip movements that cause pain, dizziness, shortness of breath, or a feeling of instability. Consider checking with a qualified clinician before starting new movement practices if you have an injury, a condition that affects balance, or other medical concerns that could make movement unsafe.

Also stop if the exercise makes you feel more activated or uncomfortable. You can switch to simply sitting with both feet on the floor and looking around the room, then return to your next small task.

If stress, anxiety, panic, or physical tension is frequently disrupting your work, sleep, relationships, or daily life, self-help exercises may not be enough. A licensed healthcare or mental health professional can help you sort out what support fits your situation. See [when to consider professional support](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional).

## How can you make this reset easier to use at work?

Do not wait for an ideal break. Use the narrow gap you already have.

Try attaching the routine to one predictable transition: immediately after a difficult call, when you close a meeting window, after sending a high-stakes message, or before entering the next room. A calendar reminder called “60-second reset” can help at first.

Keep the standard low. On a packed day, 20 seconds of loosening your hands and dropping your shoulders still counts. The purpose is not to eliminate stress before every task. It is to give your body a brief chance to change gears.

## What should you expect after one minute?

You may notice that your shoulders feel less lifted, your hands less tight, or your attention a little less stuck on the previous task. You may also notice very little. Both are possible.

Think of this as a practical transition ritual, not a test of whether you can calm yourself on command. Use the information your body gives you. If the routine helps you feel even slightly more ready for the next step, it can be a useful tool to keep nearby.
