# Breathing Exercises to Fall Back Asleep: A Gentle Slow-Exhale Method

If you are awake in the dark and your mind is starting to race, do not try to force sleep. Try this quiet breathing exercise instead. It needs no timer, screen, or special position.

The aim is simple: make your exhale a little longer than your inhale, without straining or taking huge breaths. You are giving your attention one small, repeatable job while letting your body stay still and comfortable.

## What breathing exercise can you do in bed right now?

Use a **4-in, 6-out slow-exhale breath**.

You can count silently, use the rise and fall of your belly, or simply estimate the timing. The counts are not a test. A soft, easy breath matters more than getting the numbers exactly right.

1. **Get as comfortable as you can.** Stay in the position you are already in, or place one hand lightly on your abdomen if that feels soothing. Let your jaw unclench and your shoulders drop into the mattress.

2. **Exhale normally first.** Do not push all the air out. Just let the breath leave.

3. **Breathe in gently for about 4 counts.** If breathing through your nose is comfortable, do that. If you are congested, breathe in whatever way feels easiest.

4. **Breathe out softly for about 6 counts.** Imagine you are slowly fogging a window with your mouth closed, or letting air out of a balloon very gradually. Keep the exhale smooth, not forceful.

5. **Repeat for 10 breaths.** If you are still awake, continue for another 10. There is no need to track perfectly. If counting makes you more alert, replace the numbers with the words “in” and “out.”

6. **When your thoughts interrupt, return at the exhale.** You do not need to solve the thought, argue with it, or start the count over. Notice that you drifted, then quietly come back to the feeling of the breath leaving your body.

That is the whole exercise. Your only task is a comfortable inhale and a slightly longer, unforced exhale.

## Why use a longer exhale when sleep feels far away?

When you wake up anxious, the problem is often not just being awake. It is the second layer that arrives quickly: checking the time, calculating tomorrow, scanning your body, or trying hard to make sleep happen.

A simple breathing rhythm can interrupt that loop by giving your attention a neutral physical anchor. Slow breathing may also influence physiological arousal, including breathing rate. But it is important not to turn this into a promise that one pattern will switch your nervous system off or make you sleep on command.

Research on brief breathwork is encouraging, with limits. In a 2023 randomized trial, Balban and colleagues compared five minutes a day of several breathing practices with mindfulness meditation over 28 days. The breathing groups, particularly cyclic sighing, showed improvements in mood and reductions in resting respiratory rate. This study was not a test of a middle-of-the-night sleep cure, and its breathing practices were not identical to the 4-in, 6-out rhythm above. Still, it supports the idea that brief, structured breathing can be a useful self-regulation practice for some people.

A 2023 meta-analysis by Fincham and colleagues pooled 12 randomized controlled trials involving 785 adults and found a modest reduction in self-reported stress from breathwork interventions (standardized effect size g = -0.35). The studies varied in technique, duration, and participants, so this does not prove that any single breathing count works for everyone or treats insomnia.

In other words, slow breathing is worth trying as a low-effort tool, not as a guaranteed sleep solution. If you notice yourself thinking, “This has to work now,” gently return to the next easy exhale.

## How long should you keep breathing to fall back asleep?

Try two rounds of 10 breaths. For many people, that is roughly two to four minutes, depending on the pace. Then check in without opening your eyes or reaching for your phone.

If you feel sleepier, stop counting and let your breathing become natural. You do not need to maintain a special rhythm once you are drowsy.

If you are still awake, you can do another 10 breaths. Keep the pace comfortable. Breathing more deeply or more slowly than is comfortable can make some people feel lightheaded, tingly, or more focused on their body. Ease up if that happens. A smaller breath with a modestly longer exhale is enough.

It can also help to define success more gently. The practice may not put you to sleep immediately, but it can give you a quieter place to rest than repeatedly checking the clock or rehearsing tomorrow.

If racing thoughts are the main thing keeping you awake, you might also like [how to quiet racing thoughts at night](/blog/quiet-racing-thoughts-at-night).

## What if counting makes you more awake?

Drop the numbers. Keep the shape of the exercise.

Try this cue instead:

- Breathe in, easy.
- Breathe out, a little slower.

Or place your attention only on one sensation, such as the air at your nostrils, your chest lowering, or the weight of the blanket. Each time you notice you are planning, worrying, or listening for sleep, return to that one sensation on the out-breath.

This is not about clearing your mind. Minds produce thoughts, especially at 3 a.m. The useful move is noticing the thought without following it into a full conversation.

Avoid adding extra steps, changing patterns every minute, or looking up new sleep advice on your phone. The offline simplicity is part of the point. ## Can you use 4-7-8 breathing for sleep instead?

Some people prefer **4-7-8 breathing**: inhale for 4, hold for 7, and exhale for 8. It is commonly suggested for sleep because it creates a long, deliberate exhale and gives the mind a clear counting task.

But the long hold is not necessary. If you are already anxious, the hold can feel uncomfortable or make you more aware of your breathing. Start with the gentler 4-in, 6-out pattern above, which has no breath-hold.

If you choose to try 4-7-8 breathing, keep it light and do only a few rounds. Stop if you feel dizzy, uncomfortable, or strained. **Skip long breath-holds if you are pregnant or have heart conditions.** You can always return to a comfortable, natural breath with a slightly longer exhale.

For a closer comparison of common breathing patterns, see [4-7-8 breathing vs. box breathing vs. physiological sighs](/blog).

## What should you do after the exercise?

Let go of the project of sleeping. Keep the room dark, avoid checking the time if you can, and allow your breath to return to normal.

If you are lying awake for a long time and getting increasingly frustrated, a quiet reset outside the bed can sometimes be more helpful than trying harder. Keep lights dim and choose something boring and calming until you feel sleepy again. This is a general sleep strategy, not a rule you must follow perfectly in the middle of the night.

If sleep trouble, nighttime anxiety, or panic-like symptoms are frequent, worsening, or affecting your days, it may help to speak with a clinician. You can read more about [when to see a professional](/blog). Breathing exercises are self-help tools for everyday situational anxiety and sleeplessness, not a diagnosis or a replacement for medical or mental health care.

## Can you make this easier tomorrow night?

Practice the same 4-in, 6-out rhythm once during the day, when you are not trying to fall asleep. Just 10 comfortable breaths can make the pattern feel more familiar at night.

Then, if you wake up later, you will not need to decide what technique to use. You can settle in, take one normal exhale, and begin: in for about 4, out for about 6.

The best sleep breathing exercise is usually the one you can do gently, consistently, and without turning it into another thing to get right.
