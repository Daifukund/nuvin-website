# Panic Attack Symptoms: Why Your Body Reacts This Way

Panic attack symptoms can feel sudden, intense, and deeply alarming. Your heart pounds. Your chest may feel tight. Your hands tingle. The room can seem strange or unreal.

When this is happening, try this first: do not force yourself to take a huge breath. Instead, use a gentle, longer-exhale breath for one minute.

## Can a longer exhale help when panic symptoms surge?

Sit or stand in a position that feels steady. Let your shoulders drop if they will.

1. Breathe in gently through your nose for about 3 seconds.
2. Breathe out slowly, through your nose or pursed lips, for about 4 to 6 seconds.
3. Keep the inhale small and easy. The goal is not to fill your lungs.
4. Repeat for 6 to 10 breaths.

Try silently saying, “In, easy. Out, longer.”

Panic can make you breathe faster or deeper than your body needs, even when it feels as if you cannot get enough air. A slower, unforced exhale may help interrupt that pattern. It is not a test you need to pass. If counting makes you more tense, drop the numbers and simply let the out-breath be a little longer than the in-breath.

If you feel more dizzy, uncomfortable, or focused on your breathing, return to your usual breathing and place both feet on the floor. You can also use this [step-by-step guide to stopping a panic attack](/blog/how-to-stop-a-panic-attack) when you want more support in the moment.

> **Safety note:** New, severe, unusual, or persistent symptoms need medical attention to rule out medical causes. Seek urgent care for symptoms that could be an emergency, especially severe chest pain, fainting, severe trouble breathing, or symptoms that are different from your usual pattern. Do not assume symptoms are panic without medical guidance.

## What does a panic attack feel like in the body?

A panic attack is a powerful alarm response. The body detects threat, or what it interprets as threat, and rapidly prepares you to protect yourself. This is commonly called the fight-or-flight response.

That preparation can happen even when there is no obvious danger in front of you. A stressful thought, an unfamiliar body sensation, exhaustion, caffeine, a crowded space, or a memory of a past panic episode can sometimes be enough to set the alarm off.

The alarm is designed for speed, not comfort. The brain and body prioritize action: move, escape, defend yourself, or get help. That can create a cluster of panic attack physical symptoms that feel confusing if you do not know what your body is trying to do.

Understanding the “why” does not make a frightening sensation pleasant. But it can give you a less scary explanation to return to: “My alarm system may be activated. I can slow down before I decide what this means.”

## Why does my heart race during panic?

When the fight-or-flight response switches on, stress signals prompt your cardiovascular system to prepare for action. Your heart may beat faster or harder to move blood to muscles that would be useful if you needed to run, fight, or get to safety.

You may notice pounding, fluttering, skipped-beat sensations, a pulse in your neck, or awareness of every heartbeat. Once you notice it, it is natural to check it again. That attention can make the sensation feel bigger and more urgent.

This can create a fear-of-symptoms loop:

1. You notice a change, such as a fast heartbeat.
2. Your mind interprets it as dangerous.
3. Fear increases the body’s alarm response.
4. The heartbeat and other sensations become more noticeable.
5. The sensations seem to confirm the fear.

The goal is not to argue yourself out of every thought. Try a more neutral statement: “A fast heart rate can happen when the body is alarmed. I am going to use my longer exhale and get medical help if this is new, severe, or concerning.”

Chest pressure or tightness can also occur with anxiety, but it has many possible causes. Read more about <span data-internal="anxiety-chest-tightness-symptoms">anxiety chest tightness symptoms</span>, and seek medical assessment for new or severe chest symptoms.

## Why do I feel like I cannot breathe during panic?

During panic, breathing often becomes faster, shallower, or more forceful. Some people repeatedly try to take a satisfying deep breath. Yet the harder they work to “get enough air,” the more uncomfortable their breathing can feel.

This is one reason the experience can be so confusing. The sensation is real: you may feel air hunger, throat tightness, chest tightness, or a need to yawn or sigh. But the feeling of not getting a satisfying breath does not, by itself, tell you what is causing it.

Rapid or deep breathing can change the balance of carbon dioxide in the blood. That shift can contribute to lightheadedness, tingling, and a sense that breathing is not working normally. Gentle, slower breathing is meant to reduce over-efforting, not to make you take in more air.

Because breathing problems can also have medical causes, do not use this explanation to dismiss symptoms that are new, severe, or unlike your usual experience. A clinician can help rule out medical causes.

## Why do my hands tingle during a panic attack?

Tingling or numbness in the hands, fingers, lips, face, or feet can be one of the more startling panic attack symptoms. It may occur when fast or deep breathing changes carbon dioxide levels, which can affect how nerves and blood vessels behave. Muscle tension and holding your hands tightly can add to the sensation.

This can feel especially alarming because tingling may make your hands feel unfamiliar, weak, cold, or stiff. If it happens during panic, gently uncurl your fingers and rest your palms on your thighs or another stable surface. Then return to the small-inhale, longer-exhale rhythm.

Tingling has many possible causes. It is important to have new, one-sided, persistent, or severe tingling assessed promptly, especially if it comes with weakness, confusion, trouble speaking, fainting, or other concerning symptoms.

## Why can panic make the world feel unreal?

Some people experience derealization, a sense that the surroundings seem distant, foggy, dreamlike, or unreal. Others experience depersonalization, a sense of being detached from themselves or watching themselves from the outside.

These experiences can be frightening, particularly if they are unfamiliar. During high anxiety, attention can narrow sharply toward threat and internal sensations. Stress, rapid breathing, sleep loss, and intense self-monitoring may all contribute to a strange or disconnected feeling.

Rather than trying to prove that everything feels normal, use one concrete point of contact. Notice the pressure of your feet in your shoes while you make each exhale a little longer. Name one factual detail: “The floor is firm,” or “My hands are on my knees.” This is not about forcing the feeling away. It is about giving your attention something steady to return to.

If feelings of unreality are new, persistent, distressing, or interfering with daily life, discuss them with a qualified health professional.

## Why do panic symptoms feel so dangerous when they are happening?

Your brain is built to pay close attention to possible danger. When your body is already activated, ordinary sensations can be interpreted through a threat-focused lens. A pulse becomes “something is terribly wrong.” Lightheadedness becomes “I am about to pass out.” A detached feeling becomes “I am losing control.”

Those interpretations can intensify fear, which can intensify the physical sensations. That is the fear-of-symptoms loop.

A useful alternative is not forced reassurance. It is accurate uncertainty with a plan: “This may be my alarm response, and I will take the symptoms seriously. I will slow my exhale, avoid repeated checking for the next minute, and seek medical care if these symptoms are new, severe, or concerning.”

You do not have to solve the entire experience while your nervous system is on high alert. Your next job can be very small: soften one exhale, feel the floor, and let one minute pass.

## When should I talk with a professional about panic symptoms?

Consider speaking with a doctor or mental health professional if panic symptoms are recurring, affecting work, sleep, relationships, or your willingness to leave home. Professional support can also help when you find yourself avoiding places or activities because you fear another episode.

A doctor can help rule out medical contributors to symptoms such as racing heart, breathlessness, dizziness, chest discomfort, or tingling. A mental health professional can help you understand the fear-of-symptoms cycle and build skills for responding differently over time.
