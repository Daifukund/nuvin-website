# Job Interview Anxiety: How to Calm Down in the Waiting Room

Job interview anxiety often gets loud in the last few minutes. You may be sitting outside the room, watching the door, replaying answers, or noticing your hands shake.

You do not need to feel perfectly calm to interview well. Your immediate job is smaller: give your body a quiet signal to slow down, then put your attention on the first simple thing you will say.

Try this discreet breathing reset now. It takes about a minute, needs no app or equipment, and can be done in a lobby, elevator, or bathroom stall.

## How can I calm down before an interview in one minute?

Use a **gentle double inhale followed by a long exhale**, sometimes called a physiological sigh. The aim is not to force relaxation or take huge breaths. It is to make your exhale longer and slower than your inhale for a few rounds.

1. **Set your feet down.**

   Put both feet on the floor if you can. Let your hands rest on your bag, lap, or the arms of the chair. Unclench your jaw once.

2. **Breathe in through your nose, gently.**

   Take an easy inhale, about 70 percent full. Do not gulp air.

3. **Add one small top-up inhale.**

   Without breathing out first, take a short second sip of air through your nose. This second inhale should be smaller than the first.

4. **Exhale slowly.**

   Let the air leave through your mouth or nose in one unhurried breath. Think, “long and soft,” rather than trying to empty your lungs completely. If it helps, silently count to five or six on the exhale.

5. **Pause normally, then repeat.**

   Take two or three rounds. After the third, return to your natural breathing. Keep your shoulders low.

That is the whole reset. You do not need to keep repeating it until you feel nothing. One to three gentle rounds can be enough to interrupt the rush to fix your feelings.

If the double inhale feels awkward, skip it. Simply breathe in comfortably, then make the exhale a little longer. If you feel lightheaded, stop the exercise and return to normal, easy breathing. For a more detailed guide, see [the physiological sigh technique](/blog/physiological-sigh-fastest-technique).

## What should I focus on while I breathe?

Give your mind one physical job. Anxiety before an interview often pulls attention into prediction: *What if I freeze? What if they do not like me? What if I say the wrong thing?*

During each exhale, notice one concrete point of contact:

- the soles of your shoes against the floor
- your back against the chair
- the weight of your phone or folder in your hand

Choose only one. You are not trying to prove that everything will go well. You are bringing your attention out of imagined future conversations and back to the room you are actually in.

A useful silent cue is: **“Feet down. Long exhale. One question at a time.”**

This is especially helpful because an interview is not one giant performance. It is a series of small moments: greeting someone, sitting down, hearing a question, giving one answer, then listening again.

## What do I do in the waiting room before an interview?

Use the waiting room as a transition, not a final exam.

After your two or three breathing rounds, do this simple sequence:

**Put your materials in order once.** Check your phone is silenced, your notes are accessible, and any documents you need are ready. Then stop checking. Re-reading every prepared answer can feed the sense that you are not ready yet.

**Pick one opening line.** You do not need a script for the entire interview. Choose a natural first sentence, such as: “Hi, it is great to meet you. Thanks for having me.” Having a first line ready removes one small decision from the first few seconds.

**Choose one intention that is in your control.** For example: “I will listen carefully,” “I will pause before I answer,” or “I will give clear examples.” Avoid an outcome goal such as “I must impress them” or “I must get this job.” Those goals place your nervous system in a contest with the future.

Then let yourself wait. Looking nervous, feeling alert, or having a fast heartbeat does not mean you have failed at calming down. Your task is to make room for the feeling while continuing with the next useful action.

## What should I do in the first 60 seconds of the interview?

The first minute can feel like a spotlight. Keep it deliberately ordinary.

When your interviewer arrives, stand if appropriate and let your next exhale happen before you begin speaking. Make eye contact at the level that feels comfortable, say your prepared greeting, and allow yourself to speak slightly slower than anxiety wants you to.

If you are offered water, it is fine to take it. The brief pause can help you settle in.

When the first question comes, you can use a pause. Try one of these:

- “That is a good question. Let me think for a moment.”
- “I would start with an example from my last role.”
- “Could you repeat the last part of the question?”

A short pause is professional. It gives you time to breathe out, understand the question, and organize your answer. You do not need to fill every second with words.

If you notice shaking, keep your hands lightly together on your lap, rest them on the table, or hold a pen gently. Rather than fighting the sensation, return to your cue: feet down, long exhale, one question at a time.

## Does this breathing technique actually help with stress?

There is promising research on structured breathing, but it is worth keeping the claims modest.

In a 2023 randomized trial, Balban and colleagues compared five minutes per day of different breathing practices with mindfulness meditation over 28 days. Participants assigned to cyclic sighing, a pattern that emphasizes exhalation and includes a double inhale, showed improvements in mood and reductions in resting respiratory rate relative to mindfulness in that study. It was a daily practice study, not a test of a single 60-second waiting-room reset, so it cannot guarantee an immediate result for every interview.

A 2023 meta-analysis by Fincham and colleagues reviewed 12 randomized controlled trials involving 785 adults and found that breathwork interventions were associated with lower self-reported stress than control conditions, with a small-to-moderate average effect (Hedges’ g = -0.35). The studies varied in their breathing methods, duration, and participants. That means breathwork is a reasonable low-risk coping tool for many people, not a proven switch that turns anxiety off on command.

The practical takeaway is simple: use the breath as a brief regulation practice, then return to the task in front of you. Do not judge the technique by whether you become completely calm. Judge it by whether it helps you take the next step with a little more steadiness.

## What if my interview nerves keep getting in the way?

Job interview anxiety is common, especially when the role matters. Still, you deserve more support if anxiety repeatedly leads you to avoid opportunities, severely disrupts work or daily life, or feels difficult to manage on your own. A licensed mental health professional can help you build skills that fit your situation. Read more about [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional).

For the next few minutes, keep the goal small. Feel your feet. Take one gentle double inhale and a long exhale. Say hello. Then answer the first question, not the whole interview at once.
