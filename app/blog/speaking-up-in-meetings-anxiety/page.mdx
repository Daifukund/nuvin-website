# Anxiety About Speaking Up in Meetings: A 30-Second Reset

If you are staring at the unmute button, heart racing, use this before you decide whether your comment is good enough.

## Can you reset before you speak in a meeting?

Try the **two-breath, one-sentence bridge**. It is designed for the few seconds between having a thought and saying it.

1. **Sit or stand in a steady position.** Put both feet on the floor if you can. Let your shoulders drop a little. You do not need to look calm. You are simply giving your body a more stable starting point.

2. **Take a gentle inhale through your nose.** Do not fill your lungs to the limit. Aim for a comfortable, ordinary breath.

3. **Add a small second inhale.** Take a brief top-up sip of air through your nose.

4. **Exhale slowly and easily.** Breathe out through your mouth, a little longer than the inhale. Let your jaw and hands soften as you exhale.

5. **Repeat once, if you have time.** Keep both breaths gentle. Do not rush or force them.

6. **Unmute or raise your hand before you start evaluating your comment again.** Then use one simple opening sentence: “I have one thought to add,” “Can I ask a clarifying question?” or “My read is…”

That is the bridge: two slow breaths, then one sentence. The goal is not to erase anxiety. It is to make enough room to take the next small action while anxiety is present.

If a second inhale or a long exhale makes you lightheaded or uncomfortable, return to your normal breathing. You can still use the action part: feet down, unmute, one sentence.

## Why does speaking up feel so hard right before you do it?

Anxiety about speaking up in meetings often has less to do with having nothing useful to say and more to do with being visible. Your brain may treat the moment as a social evaluation: people might disagree, notice a mistake, interrupt, or judge how you sound.

Meetings add pressure because the timing is tight. You may be trying to listen, organize a thought, read other people’s reactions, decide whether the point is worth making, and find a gap in the conversation, all at once. On video calls, the unmute button can become its own tiny threshold.

When this happens, it is common to wait for certainty. But certainty is rarely available in a live meeting. The longer you wait for the perfect wording, the more your body can interpret the delay as evidence that the moment is dangerous.

The two-breath, one-sentence bridge interrupts that loop. It gives you a defined, short task instead of asking you to become confident on command.

## What should you say after you unmute?

Choose a sentence frame before the meeting starts. This is not a script for your whole contribution. It is just a low-pressure way to begin before your anxious mind can negotiate you out of speaking.

Pick one that fits your situation:

- “I want to build on that point.”
- “Can I check my understanding?”
- “One risk I am noticing is…”
- “I have a quick question about…”
- “My recommendation would be…”

Then make the first sentence your only job.

You do not have to deliver a polished mini-presentation. You can pause after your opening. You can look at your notes. You can say, “Let me put that more clearly.” Most meetings have room for ordinary human pauses, even if anxiety tells you otherwise.

For example, if you are scared to talk in meetings because you think your idea is incomplete, try: “This is an early thought, but I am wondering whether we should consider…” That is a contribution, not a promise that you have solved everything.

## How do you use this when your body feels panicky?

Make the technique smaller, not more intense.

When meeting anxiety is high, people sometimes take big, rapid breaths in an effort to calm down. That can feel uncomfortable or make lightheadedness worse. Instead, keep the inhale modest and let the exhale be unhurried. Think “soften” rather than “breathe deeply.”

As you exhale, place attention on one physical contact point: your feet in your shoes, your back against the chair, or your fingertips against the desk. This is not about forcing yourself to relax. It is a brief cue that you are here, in a chair, at a meeting, doing one manageable thing.

Then move quickly to the action. Click unmute. Raise your hand. Type the first line in chat if that is the appropriate way to enter the conversation.

A useful rule is: **breathe twice, then begin before composing a third internal draft.**

## Does the breathing part have evidence behind it?

Brief breathing practices may help with stress and mood, but they are not a guaranteed switch that turns anxiety off.

A Stanford randomized trial by Balban and colleagues compared brief daily structured breathing practices with mindfulness meditation. The cyclic sighing practice, which uses an inhale, a second shorter inhale, and a longer exhale, was associated with improvements in mood and lower resting respiratory rate over the study period. The study involved regular practice, not a test of speaking up in a meeting, so it cannot tell us that two breaths will reliably make a meeting feel easy. [Balban et al., 2023, *Cell Reports Medicine*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)

A 2023 meta-analysis by Fincham and colleagues examined 12 randomized controlled trials involving 785 adults. It found a small-to-moderate average reduction in self-reported stress from breathwork interventions, with an effect size of g = -0.35. The studies used different breathing methods and had limitations, so this is promising evidence, not proof that any specific breath pattern works for every person or situation.

The practical takeaway is modest: a gentle, structured breath may be a useful pause before a hard social action. It is not a test of willpower, a cure for anxiety, or a replacement for care when anxiety is persistently interfering with your life.

## What if you are afraid to speak in meetings because you might sound nervous?

Try not to make “sounding calm” the condition for speaking. Anxiety can make you monitor your voice, face, and wording so closely that you lose the thread of what you wanted to say.

Aim for useful, not flawless. A slightly shaky voice can still ask a clear question. A brief pause can still make a strong point. People are usually focused on the meeting content and their own next contribution more than on the details you are reviewing afterward.

## What do you do after you have spoken?

Your brain may immediately replay the moment: “That was awkward,” “I talked too much,” or “They probably disagreed.” Rather than debating every thought, use a short closing statement: **“I contributed. Back to the meeting.”**

Then put your attention on the next speaker, your notes, or one concrete agenda item. This is not pretending you had a perfect experience. It is declining to hold a full performance review while the meeting is still happening.

Afterward, write down only the facts: what you said, what happened next, and one thing you would repeat or adjust next time. Facts are usually more useful than an anxiety-fueled verdict.

## When is it worth getting more support?

If anxiety speaking up in meetings is regularly affecting your work, relationships, sleep, or choices, support from a qualified mental health professional can help. You do not have to wait until it feels unbearable. Read more about [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional).

For today, keep the target small: two gentle breaths, unmute, one sentence. You are practicing participation, not trying to prove that you never feel anxious.
