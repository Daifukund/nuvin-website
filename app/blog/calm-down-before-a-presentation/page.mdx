# How to Calm Down Before a Presentation: A 5-Minute Breathing Routine

If you have five minutes before a meeting, do this one thing: take a normal inhale through your nose, add a small second inhale, then let out a long, unforced exhale.

Repeat at an easy pace for up to five minutes.

This is called **cyclic sighing**, a structured breathing pattern that can be subtle enough to do at your desk, in a hallway, or in a bathroom stall. It will not make a meaningful presentation feel meaningless. The goal is smaller and more useful: give your body a brief, practical cue to slow down so you can walk in and speak from a steadier place.

## What should you do in the five minutes before you walk in?

Set a timer for five minutes, or simply continue until it is time to go. Put both feet on the floor if you are sitting. Let your shoulders drop as much as they comfortably can. You do not need a perfect posture.

Then use this rhythm:

1. **Inhale gently through your nose.** Let the breath fill your lungs comfortably, without trying to take the biggest breath possible.
2. **Take one small top-up inhale.** It is a short second sip of air through the nose, not another full breath.
3. **Exhale slowly and easily.** Breathe out through your mouth, or through gently pursed lips if that feels more discreet. Let the exhale be longer than the two inhales together.
4. **Pause only naturally.** When the exhale ends, begin the next inhale. Do not hold your breath or force an empty feeling.

That is one cycle.

Your main job is to keep the exhale relaxed and longer. Think, “in, little top-up, long out.” If counting helps, you might inhale for about two to three seconds, take a quick top-up, and exhale for about five to eight seconds. But comfort matters more than counting. A strained or overly long breath is not the point.

In a shared workspace, no one needs to know what you are doing. Keep your mouth closed on the exhale if that is more comfortable, or make the out-breath quiet. You can look at your notes, your calendar, or a neutral spot on the wall while you practice.

## How can you make cyclic sighing feel discreet at your desk or in the bathroom?

Presentation anxiety often comes with a feeling of urgency. Your brain may be racing through slides, imagining questions, or replaying what could go wrong. That urgency can make a breathing exercise feel like one more performance task.

Make this as low-key as possible.

At your desk, place your hands in your lap or rest them on the table. Let your screen stay open if you need to look occupied. Your chest and shoulders do not need to visibly rise. A soft inhale and an even softer top-up are enough.

In a bathroom, you can stand with your back supported by a wall or sit if there is a safe, private place to do so. Unclench your jaw before you begin. A clenched jaw can make both breathing and speaking feel harder than they need to.

You do not need to close your eyes. In fact, keeping them open can make this easier when you are about to return to a busy room. Pick one ordinary visual detail, such as the edge of a mirror, a tile, or the corner of your slide deck. Let it be there while you breathe. The task is not to clear your mind. The task is to keep returning to the next long exhale.

If people are waiting for you, even 60 to 90 seconds can be a useful reset. If you have the full five minutes, use them. Try not to spend the last minute checking whether your heart rate, hands, or face have changed. Instead, let the final cycle end, stand up, and go.

## What should the five-minute routine look like minute by minute?

A time box helps because it prevents the familiar pre-presentation spiral of trying to fix every feeling before you enter. You are not waiting to become perfectly calm. You are giving yourself a short runway.

**Minute 1: Find an easy rhythm.**

Your first few breaths may feel choppy. That is okay. Do not compensate by taking huge breaths. Use a smaller, gentler inhale and a long exhale. The first minute is simply about getting into the pattern.

A helpful cue is: “Long out, no rush.”

**Minutes 2 and 3: Keep the exhale unforced.**

This is the middle of the practice, when the mind may start objecting: “This will not be enough,” “I need to review that slide,” or “What if my voice shakes?” Notice the thought, then return to the next cycle.

You do not have to argue with the thought or replace it with a positive one. Your presentation can still matter. You can still prefer to do well. Just avoid turning the next two minutes into a mental rehearsal of disaster.

**Minute 4: Let your face and throat soften.**

Continue exactly the same breathing pattern. On each exhale, see whether you can let your tongue rest away from the roof of your mouth and loosen the muscles around your eyes. Do not stretch or force anything.

This is not a separate exercise. It is just a reminder that you do not have to brace every part of your body while you breathe.

**Minute 5: Prepare to enter while still breathing normally.**

For the last few cycles, picture only the first physical action you will take: opening the door, joining the call, standing at the front, or saying hello. Not the entire presentation. Just the first action.

When it is time, stop deliberately. Take one normal breath. Pick up your materials and move toward the room or meeting link.

The aim is to carry a little of the slower pace into your first sentence, rather than trying to remain in a special calm state.

## Why might a longer exhale help before public speaking?

Breathing is connected to the body systems involved in arousal and recovery. Slowing and structuring the breath may help some people feel less activated in the moment. A long exhale is a simple feature you can control when your thoughts are not especially cooperative.

There is meaningful, but limited, research behind structured breathwork. In a 2023 randomized trial, Balban and colleagues compared five-minute daily breathing practices over 28 days. Participants assigned to cyclic sighing showed improvements in mood and reductions in respiratory rate, compared with a mindfulness meditation group in that study. The study is published in *Cell Reports Medicine* ([DOI: 10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)).

A 2023 meta-analysis by Fincham and colleagues, published in *Scientific Reports*, reviewed 12 randomized controlled trials involving 785 adults. It found a small-to-moderate average reduction in self-reported stress from breathwork interventions, with an effect size of g = -0.35.

These findings are encouraging, not a guarantee. The research does not show that one five-minute session will erase presentation anxiety, prevent every physical symptom, or make anyone a confident speaker on demand. Studies of breathwork also vary in the techniques used, the people studied, and the outcomes measured. Breathwork can be helpful without deserving the hype sometimes attached to it.

For the minutes before you speak, its advantage is practical: it is brief, free, portable, and does not require you to solve the entire situation first.

## What if the breathing makes you feel more tense or lightheaded?

Stop trying to match a count. Return to ordinary breathing and let your breath settle on its own.

Some people feel uncomfortable when they breathe too deeply, too quickly, or too forcefully. Cyclic sighing should be gentle. The top-up inhale is small, and the exhale is slow, not squeezed out. You should not be gulping air, repeatedly holding your breath, or pushing through dizziness.

If this pattern does not feel good today, it is fine to skip it. Put both feet on the floor and breathe normally while noticing a few neutral details around you. For a separate, desk-friendly reset, try [discreet grounding at your desk](/blog/discreet-grounding-at-your-desk).

The goal is not to win at breathing. It is to arrive with a little more room to choose your next sentence.

## How can you use this when your voice is shaking?

A shaky voice can be especially frustrating because it can feel visible, even when other people are focused on the content of what you are saying. This breathing routine may help you approach your opening with less physical urgency, but it cannot promise that your voice will not tremble.

Use the final two or three breathing cycles to prepare for your **first sentence only**. Then, when you begin, let yourself speak that sentence a little more slowly than your anxious urge suggests. A brief pause after the first sentence is allowed. You do not need to fill every second with words.

If your voice wobbles, keep going with the point you planned to make. Trying to hide every sign of nerves can pull attention away from the meeting itself. Your job is not to sound perfectly untouched by the moment. Your job is to communicate.

If speaking up feels difficult even outside formal presentations, [speaking up in meetings when anxiety takes over](/blog/speaking-up-in-meetings-anxiety) may help you practice in lower-stakes moments.

## What should you tell yourself before you go in?

Keep the self-talk plain and believable. You do not need to tell yourself that there is nothing to fear, or that everyone will love your presentation. Those statements can feel unconvincing when your body is buzzing.

Try one sentence that acknowledges the moment and gives you a next step:

> “I can feel nervous and still begin.”

Pair it with the last long exhale. Then move.

This matters because waiting for anxiety to disappear can accidentally teach your brain that you need complete calm before you can participate. Most people do not. You can bring nerves into the room and still make eye contact, explain a chart, answer a question, or introduce an idea.

## When is it worth getting more support for presentation anxiety?

A five-minute breathing practice is a self-help tool for everyday situational anxiety. It is not therapy or a diagnosis, and it does not have to be your only support.

Consider talking with a qualified health professional if fear of meetings or public speaking is regularly causing you to avoid important work, school, or social opportunities, if it is getting worse, or if self-guided strategies are not enough. For today, keep it small: one gentle inhale, one small top-up, and one long exhale. Then take the next step into the room.

## Can you practice this before you need it?

Yes. Practicing during an ordinary, low-pressure moment can make the sequence easier to remember when a meeting is about to start. Try one or two minutes while waiting for your coffee, before opening email, or after you sit down at your desk.

You do not need to turn it into a major routine. Familiarity is the benefit. The less you have to figure out in the five minutes before a presentation, the more likely you are to use the technique gently.
