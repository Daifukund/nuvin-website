# How to Stop a Panic Attack: A Step-by-Step Script for Right Now

If panic is rising right now, do not try to solve everything at once. Start with the next breath and the next thing you can notice.

This is one simple sequence: gentle box breathing, followed by 5-4-3-2-1 grounding. The goal is not to force yourself to feel calm instantly. The goal is to give your attention and body a steady task while the surge passes.

> **Safety note:** Panic attacks themselves are not dangerous, but first-time symptoms or chest pain warrant a medical checkup. New, severe, or concerning chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, or symptoms that feel medically urgent should be assessed urgently. Do not use an article to decide whether a new physical symptom is panic.

## Can you begin this panic-attack script right now?

Yes. Read one numbered step, then do only that step. You do not need to do it perfectly.

### 1. Put yourself somewhere as physically steady as possible

If you are standing, sit down if you can. Put both feet on the floor, or lean your back against a wall or chair. Unclench your hands if they are tightly curled.

Look at one stable object in the room, such as a doorframe, a table, or a corner of the ceiling. You are not trying to make the sensations disappear. You are giving yourself a fixed point while they are here.

Say quietly, in your head or out loud:

> “I am having a strong wave of panic. I only need to do the next step.”

Use words that feel believable. You do not have to convince yourself that everything is fine. A small, accurate statement is enough: *This is intense, and I am taking one steady action.*

### 2. Do four gentle box breaths

Box breathing means breathing in, pausing, breathing out, and pausing for roughly equal counts. Keep the breaths light. This is not a contest to take the deepest breath possible.

Follow this pattern four times:

1. Breathe in through your nose for **four** slow counts.
2. Pause gently for **four** counts.
3. Breathe out, softly, for **four** counts.
4. Pause gently for **four** counts.

You can count in your head: *in, 2, 3, 4. Hold, 2, 3, 4. Out, 2, 3, 4. Pause, 2, 3, 4.*

Let your shoulders stay low. Let your jaw be loose. On the out-breath, imagine you are slowly fogging a mirror, but keep the breath quiet and easy.

If a four-count hold makes you more dizzy, air-hungry, or uncomfortable, change the pattern. Try breathing in for three and out for four, with no pauses. Or simply make your out-breath a little longer than your in-breath. You are allowed to adapt the exercise. For some people in a panic surge, trying to control breathing too tightly can feel worse.

The important part is the gentle rhythm, not the shape of the box.

### 3. Keep the breathing light and name five things you can see

After four rounds, let your breathing return to its normal pace. Keep it easy. Now look around and name **five things you can see**.

Be specific and plain:

- “A blue mug.”
- “A scratch on the table.”
- “A shadow near the window.”
- “My left shoe.”
- “The edge of a picture frame.”

Try not to evaluate the things you see. You do not need to find pleasant objects. Your only job is to notice details that are actually present.

This shifts attention from scanning your body and thoughts for danger to observing the environment in front of you. If your attention snaps back to symptoms, that is okay. Gently return to the next object.

### 4. Name four things you can physically feel

Now identify **four things you can feel through touch or pressure**.

For example:

- “My feet pressing into the floor.”
- “The chair supporting my back.”
- “The fabric of my sleeve.”
- “Cool air on my hands.”

Press your feet down for one slow out-breath, then release the pressure. You are not trying to push panic out of your body. You are noticing that your body is in contact with something solid, right here.

If touch feels overwhelming, make this step simpler. Name one point of contact, such as your heel against the floor, and stay with it for three breaths.

### 5. Name three sounds, two smells, and one taste

Continue the same grounding sequence.

Name **three sounds**. They can be very quiet: a fan, traffic, a refrigerator hum, your own breathing, or the fabric moving as you shift.

Then name **two smells**. If you cannot notice a smell, name two neutral scents you expect in the space, such as soap or fresh air. You can also hold a familiar object nearby, such as a sleeve, tea, or lotion, and notice its scent without taking a sharp inhale.

Finally, name **one taste**. Maybe it is toothpaste, water, gum, or simply the taste already in your mouth. If possible, take one small sip of water and notice the temperature.

That is the full 5-4-3-2-1 sequence. You have not failed if panic is still present. You have interrupted the urge to fight, predict, and monitor every sensation for a few minutes. That matters.

For a version that focuses more closely on this practice, read [5-4-3-2-1 grounding when panicking](/blog).

### 6. Repeat only the part that helped most

Pause and ask one practical question: *Which part gave me even 1% more room?*

Maybe the counted exhale helped. Maybe pressing your feet into the floor helped. Maybe naming objects helped because it gave your mind something concrete to do.

Repeat that part for two more minutes.

If nothing felt helpful yet, choose the simplest version: keep your feet supported, look at one object, and make the next out-breath slightly slower. Panic can make improvement hard to detect in the moment. You do not need proof that the method is working before you continue gently.

## What should you do if the panic does not stop immediately?

A panic attack does not always switch off after one grounding exercise. Treat this sequence as a way to ride the wave, not a button that guarantees instant calm.

Keep the next ten minutes small. Avoid making big decisions, searching symptoms repeatedly, or demanding certainty from yourself. If you are somewhere safe, stay seated or take a slow, ordinary walk around the room. Put a hand on a cool surface. Send a brief message to someone you trust: “I am having a hard moment. Can you stay on the phone with me for a few minutes?”

If you are driving, pull over somewhere safe before doing any breathing or grounding exercise. Focus first on getting to a safe stopping place.

It can also help to loosen the goal. Rather than asking, “How do I stop panic right now?” try, “How do I get through the next 60 seconds?” Often, a less forceful goal leaves more room for your nervous system to settle. You [do not have to calm down instantly](/blog).

## Why might breathing and grounding help during panic?

Panic often pulls attention inward. You may notice a racing heart, tight chest, tingling, dizziness, or frightening thoughts, then begin checking whether those sensations are changing. A paced breathing task and a sensory task offer an alternative focus that is concrete, repeatable, and available almost anywhere.

There is meaningful research on brief breathing practices, but it is important not to exaggerate what it shows. In a randomized trial, [Balban and colleagues (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895) studied brief daily breathwork practices and mindfulness. The study found benefits for mood and physiological arousal, with a cyclic-sighing practice showing particularly strong results in that study. It did **not** test box breathing as a way to stop an active panic attack in the moment.

A 2023 meta-analysis by Fincham and colleagues in *Scientific Reports* included 12 randomized controlled trials with 785 adults. It found a small-to-moderate average reduction in self-reported stress from breathwork interventions (standardized effect size, g = -0.35). That result supports breathwork as a potentially useful self-regulation practice, but it does not mean one breathing pattern works for everyone, or that it replaces assessment and treatment for recurrent panic.

The evidence for 5-4-3-2-1 is less about proving one exact set of numbers is uniquely effective. It is a practical grounding format that directs attention toward present sensory information. If counting five things feels too complicated during a surge, reduce it. Name one thing you see, one thing you feel, and one sound you hear. Simple is still grounding.

## When should you get panic attack help beyond this script?

Use this script for an immediate moment, then consider what support would make future moments less frightening.

A medical clinician can help assess new or changing symptoms, especially if this is your first episode, you have chest pain, or you have a health condition that could affect breathing or heart symptoms. It is also worth speaking with a clinician or mental health professional if episodes keep happening, you are avoiding work, school, driving, exercise, or social situations because of fear, or you are relying on alcohol or substances to get through them.

Panic symptoms can be very real and very disruptive, and support can be useful even when you are managing day to day. Learn more about [when to see a professional](/blog).

If you are at risk of harming yourself, feel unable to stay safe, or need immediate crisis support, contact local emergency services. In the United States, you can call or text [988](https://988lifeline.org/) for the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. If you are elsewhere, contact your local crisis service or emergency number.

## What can you remember for the next wave?

You do not need to memorize a perfect script. Keep this short version:

> **Feet down. Four gentle breaths. Five things I see. Four things I feel. Three sounds. Two smells. One taste. Repeat the easiest part.**

Practice the sequence once when you are relatively calm, too. Familiarity can make it easier to use when your mind is racing.
