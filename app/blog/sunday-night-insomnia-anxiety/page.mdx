# Sunday Night Insomnia: A 5-Minute Breathing Reset for Pre-Week Anxiety

If you have Sunday night insomnia, do not try to solve your whole week from bed. For the next five minutes, give your body one simple signal: the immediate moment is safe enough to soften.

Try **cyclic sighing**, a gentle breathing pattern with two easy inhales and one longer exhale. The aim is not to force sleep. It is to turn down some of the physical activation that can keep Sunday-scaries thoughts running.

## Can you try this 5-minute reset right now?

Sit supported in bed, in a chair, or somewhere dim and comfortable. If lying in bed makes you feel as if you are working hard at sleep, sitting up elsewhere may feel easier.

Set a five-minute timer, then repeat this cycle at a comfortable pace:

1. **Breathe in gently through your nose.** Let it be an ordinary, comfortable inhale.
2. **Take a second, smaller inhale.** This is a brief top-up of air, not a big gasp.
3. **Exhale slowly and easily.** Breathe out through your mouth or nose, whichever feels more natural. Let the exhale be longer than the combined inhales.
4. **Pause naturally, then begin again.** Do not hold your breath or chase a precise count.

A useful inner cue is: *in, little in, long out.*

Keep your shoulders loose. Your exhale does not need to be dramatic. A quiet, unforced breath is enough. If your mind jumps to Monday, notice that it jumped, then return to the next long exhale.

If you feel dizzy, tingly, strained, or more uncomfortable, stop the exercise and return to normal breathing. Breathing practices should feel gentle, not like a test of endurance.

When the timer ends, do not check whether you are sleepy yet. Simply notice one neutral fact, such as the weight of the blanket or the contact of your feet with the floor. Then let yourself rest.

## Why can Sunday night insomnia feel so intense?

Sunday night can carry a particular kind of pressure. The weekend is ending, routines are about to restart, and your brain may begin scanning ahead for emails, meetings, unfinished tasks, family demands, or the commute. Even a week you can handle can feel enormous at 11 p.m.

This is often called the Sunday scaries, or anticipatory anxiety. Anticipation can activate the body before anything difficult has actually happened. That can mean a faster heartbeat, tense muscles, a busy mind, or the frustrating sense that you are tired but cannot sleep.

The thought, “I have to sleep or tomorrow will be awful,” can add a second layer of pressure. It turns sleep, which cannot be forced on command, into a deadline.

You do not need to argue with every thought tonight. The breathing practice gives your attention a smaller job: make the next exhale a little longer and easier. For more on why future-focused worry can feel so urgent, read [Sunday scaries and anticipatory anxiety](/blog/sunday-scaries-anticipatory-anxiety).

## How does a longer exhale help with work anxiety before bed?

Slow, structured breathing may help reduce physiological arousal for some people. Cyclic sighing specifically uses a longer exhale, which can be a practical way to shift from bracing toward settling.

In a randomized trial, Balban and colleagues compared brief daily breathing practices and mindfulness practice. Cyclic sighing was associated with improvements in mood and reductions in resting respiratory rate over the study period. The study did not test Sunday night insomnia specifically, and it does not mean this exercise will make everyone fall asleep quickly. Still, it supports using brief, gentle breathing as one reasonable tool for everyday stress.

The important part tonight is the intention. You are not trying to breathe perfectly, erase your work concerns, or guarantee eight hours of sleep. You are practicing a less activated response while the concerns are present.

If the thought “I cannot sleep Sunday night” appears, try adding a few words to the rhythm:

- On the first inhale: *Here.*
- On the small second inhale: *Still here.*
- On the long exhale: *Not solving Monday now.*

Then let the words go. Return to the physical pattern rather than debating whether the statement is true.

## What should you do when racing thoughts interrupt the exercise?

Expect interruptions. Racing thoughts are not proof that you are doing it wrong.

When you catch yourself planning, rehearsing a conversation, or calculating how little sleep remains, use a simple redirect: name it quietly as *planning* or *worrying*, then begin the next cycle. You do not have to finish the thought before returning to your breath.

If a task feels genuinely important, keep a notepad nearby and write only a short placeholder, such as “email Sam Monday” or “check calendar at 9.” Then come back to the same breathing pattern. The goal is not to create a complete plan. It is to show your mind that the task has a place to go other than repeated rehearsal in the dark.

Avoid watching the clock while you practice. Clock-checking often turns a few wakeful minutes into a performance review of the night.

For more guidance on responding to looping thoughts without fighting them, see [how to quiet racing thoughts at night](/blog/quiet-racing-thoughts-at-night).

## How can you make this a Sunday wind-down, not an emergency fix?

Use the five-minute practice at roughly the same point in your Sunday evening, ideally before you are deeply frustrated in bed. You might do it after setting out what you need for Monday, after brushing your teeth, or when you first notice the familiar pre-week tightening.

Keeping it brief matters. A five-minute reset is easier to repeat than an elaborate routine, especially when you are already drained. It also helps separate the practice from the demand to sleep immediately.

Think of success in small terms:

- You paused instead of spiraling for five minutes.
- You noticed a worry without following it all the way through.
- You gave your body a quieter cue before bed.

Sleep may follow quickly, slowly, or not yet. The practice can still be useful because it changes what you do during the anxious part of the night.

## What does the research actually say about breathwork for stress?

The evidence is encouraging, but it is not a promise.

Balban et al. studied structured respiration practices in a 2023 randomized trial published in *Cell Reports Medicine* (DOI: [10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)). Their findings suggest that brief breathing practices, including cyclic sighing, can help with mood and physiological arousal in the studied group.

A 2023 meta-analysis by Fincham and colleagues in *Scientific Reports* pooled 12 randomized controlled trials involving 785 adults. It found a small average reduction in self-reported stress from breathwork compared with control conditions (Hedges' g = -0.35).

Those findings do not establish that breathwork treats insomnia, anxiety disorders, or every person's Sunday-night dread. Breathwork studies use different techniques, durations, and comparison groups, and people respond differently. It is best viewed as a low-burden self-help practice that may help you settle, not a cure or a reason to ignore ongoing sleep problems.

## When is it worth getting more support for sleep or anxiety?

Consider talking with a healthcare professional if trouble sleeping is frequent, lasts for weeks, is affecting daily functioning, or comes with anxiety that feels hard to manage on your own. A clinician can help assess possible contributors and discuss evidence-based support.

It is also reasonable to seek support if Sunday dread is expanding into anxiety across much of the week, avoidance of work or daily responsibilities, or persistent low mood. You deserve more than getting through each Sunday night. Read [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional) for signs that extra support could help.

For tonight, keep the target small: one comfortable breath cycle, then another. Monday does not need to be solved before sleep.
