# Shaking to Release Tension: What It Can Help With, and What It Can’t Do

If your body feels wound up right now, try this gentle shake-and-stretch reset. The aim is simple: move some of the physical tension that can build up when you have been sitting still, bracing, rushing, or feeling anxious. It is not a test, and you do not need to shake hard for it to count.

## Can you try a 90-second shake-and-stretch reset now?

Choose a spot where you have enough room to move your arms. You can stand, or sit with both feet on the floor. Keep your movements small and comfortable.

1. **Notice your starting point for one breath.**

   Let your shoulders be where they are. Notice whether your hands are clenched, your jaw is tight, or your stomach feels braced. You do not need to fix any of it yet.

2. **Shake out your hands for 15 seconds.**

   Let your wrists stay loose and flick your hands gently, as if you were drying off water. Keep your elbows soft. A light, loose movement is enough.

3. **Let the movement travel up your arms for 15 seconds.**

   Gently sway or wiggle your forearms, shoulders, and upper body. Think “loosen,” not “push through.” If shaking feels awkward, slowly roll your shoulders instead.

4. **Pause and make your exhale a little longer.**

   Breathe in normally. Then breathe out slowly, as if you were cooling a hot drink. Do this twice. There is no need to take a huge breath or hold your breath.

5. **Add one easy stretch for 20 to 30 seconds.**

   Reach your arms overhead only as far as is comfortable, then lower them. Or clasp your hands in front of you and gently round your upper back. Keep your face, neck, and knees relaxed.

6. **Finish with your feet.**

   Press both feet gently into the floor for three seconds, then release. Look around the room and name one ordinary thing you can see. This gives your attention a clear place to land after moving.

Ask yourself one practical question: **Do I feel even 5% less braced than before?** You might notice warmer hands, a lower shoulder position, easier breathing, or simply a shift in attention. You may also notice very little. Both responses are normal.

You can repeat the sequence once if it feels good. Stop if you feel pain, dizziness, unsteadiness, or worse discomfort. A reset should be gentle enough that your body does not have to fight it.

## Why might shaking and stretching feel relieving?

Stress and anxiety can come with very physical habits: raised shoulders, a held breath, a clenched jaw, restless legs, or muscles that stay ready for action long after you need them to. Gentle movement gives those muscles something else to do.

Shaking and stretching may help through a few ordinary pathways:

- They interrupt a fixed, braced posture.
- They shift attention toward sensations you can influence right now.
- They can make it easier to notice the difference between tightening and softening.
- They provide a brief break from repetitive thoughts or a stressful task.

This is a tension-release movement, not a way to force your nervous system into calm. You may still feel anxious afterward. The useful goal is smaller and more realistic: create a little more physical room to choose what you do next.

For example, after a difficult email, you might shake out your hands, let your shoulders drop, and then return to the message with slightly less urge to react immediately. That is a meaningful use of the technique, even if the stressful situation itself has not changed.

## Does shaking release stress according to research?

The honest answer is: there is more support for related practices than for shaking itself.

Research on **progressive muscle relaxation**, or PMR, is stronger than research on free-form shaking. PMR involves deliberately tensing and then releasing muscle groups, which helps people practice recognizing physical tension. A quantitative review of abbreviated PMR reported a moderate average benefit, around *d* = 0.57, across the outcomes studied. That does not mean PMR works equally well for every person or every kind of anxiety, and study quality and methods vary. Still, it gives a reasonable evidence-based foundation for the broader idea that working with muscle tension can be helpful.

If you would rather use a more structured method, try this <span data-internal="progressive-muscle-relaxation-routine">progressive muscle relaxation routine</span>. It is often easier to evaluate because the steps are specific and repeatable.

There is also research on a single bout of physical activity and immediate anxiety. A 2015 meta-analysis by Ensari and colleagues found a small average reduction in state anxiety after acute exercise, about *g* = 0.16. That is a modest effect, not a cure, and the studies examined exercise rather than a few seconds of shaking your hands. But it supports the cautious idea that brief movement can sometimes shift how anxious a person feels in the moment.

Direct evidence for shaking practices is limited. If shaking helps you, the most defensible explanation is that it may function as gentle movement, a break in muscle bracing, and a focus for attention. It is not established as a distinct treatment with reliably predictable results.

**Research mentioned**

- Carlson, C. R., and Hoyle, R. H. (1993). *Efficacy of abbreviated progressive muscle relaxation training: A quantitative review of behavior therapy research.* Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology.
- Ensari, I., Greenlee, T. A., Motl, R. W., and Petruzzello, S. J. (2015). *Meta-analysis of acute exercise effects on state anxiety: An update of randomized controlled trials over the past 25 years.* Depression and Anxiety.

## Is a TRE technique the same thing as a simple shake reset?

Not exactly. TRE, often expanded as Tension and Trauma Releasing Exercises, is a branded or named approach that uses a sequence of exercises intended to bring on involuntary shaking or tremoring. Some people report that it feels calming or relieving.

However, broad claims that TRE or shaking can “release trauma” from the body go beyond the available evidence. Trauma is not a substance stored in muscles that movement has been proven to discharge. Shaking is not a proven trauma treatment, and it should not be presented as a replacement for trauma-focused mental health care.

A brief, voluntary shake-and-stretch reset is different in both scope and goal. You are choosing a comfortable movement to address present-moment tightness. You do not need to chase tremors, intense emotion, memories, or a dramatic release for the exercise to be worthwhile.

If a movement practice brings up distressing memories, feels overwhelming, or leaves you more activated, stop. Consider discussing that response with a qualified mental health professional rather than trying to push through it alone.

## How is shaking different from progressive muscle relaxation?

Both can be used to work with physical tension, but they ask your body to do different things.

With shaking and stretching, you use loose movement. It can be fast, informal, and useful when you need a short reset between tasks. A [60-second reset between tasks](/blog/60-second-reset-between-tasks) may fit especially well when you do not have the privacy or time for a longer practice.

With PMR, you deliberately tighten a muscle group briefly, then let it soften. The contrast can teach you what tension feels like, which is helpful when you have become so accustomed to bracing that you barely notice it.

Neither method needs to “work” perfectly. You might prefer movement on one day and PMR on another. Use the approach that feels safe, manageable, and physically comfortable, rather than trying to produce a particular sensation.

## What can shaking and stretching not do?

A shake-and-stretch reset can support everyday coping. It cannot diagnose why you feel tense, resolve an unsafe situation, or reliably stop a panic attack, chronic anxiety condition, depression, trauma-related symptoms, or another health concern.

It also cannot prove that your body has processed stress because you yawned, cried, trembled, or felt tired afterward. Those experiences can happen for many reasons. They are not a measure of whether you have healed or whether you should keep going.

Think of this practice as one small regulation tool, similar to standing up after a long meeting or unclenching your hands before a hard conversation. It may lower the volume of physical tension briefly. It does not have to carry the full weight of solving what is causing that tension.

If anxiety, fear, sleep problems, avoidance, or physical tension are regularly interfering with your life, it may help to read about [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional). Support can include a conversation with a healthcare or mental health professional, alongside any self-help skills that work for you.

## How can you use this without turning it into another task?

Keep the bar low. Try the same 90-second sequence at a predictable transition, such as after commuting, before opening a demanding message, or when you stand up from your desk. You do not need special equipment, a certain emotional state, or a dramatic result.

The question is not “Did I release all my stress?” A more useful question is, “Did this give me a small pause and reduce some bracing?” If yes, it is doing enough.
