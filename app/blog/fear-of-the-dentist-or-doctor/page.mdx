# Dentist Anxiety: How to Calm Down in the Waiting Room

If you are in a waiting room right now, do not try to make yourself feel completely fearless. Give yourself one small job: make your next exhale longer than your inhale.

This can be useful for dentist anxiety, doctor anxiety, and medical appointment anxiety because it is quiet, private, and possible even when you are already keyed up.

## What can you do right now for dentist anxiety?

Try a **physiological sigh**, then repeat it slowly for one to three minutes.

1. Let your shoulders drop if they are lifted.
2. Breathe in through your nose.
3. Before exhaling, take a second, smaller sip of air in through your nose.
4. Exhale slowly and fully through your mouth, as if you are gently cooling hot tea.
5. Pause for a moment, then repeat.

The important part is not getting the breath perfect. It is allowing a longer, unhurried exhale. Keep the breaths comfortable. Do not force a huge inhale or exhale, and return to normal breathing if you feel lightheaded or uncomfortable.

Try five rounds first. Then notice one neutral detail around you: the feeling of your feet in your shoes, the fabric of the chair, or a color on the wall. You do not need to scan your body for whether the anxiety is gone. Your task is simply to do the next round.

If counting helps, use this gentle rhythm: inhale, small second inhale, then a long exhale. Do not hold your breath between steps. A quiet exhale is enough, especially if other people are nearby.

You can read a fuller guide to the [physiological sigh](/blog/physiological-sigh-fastest-technique) if you want to practice it before your next appointment.

## Why use this breathing pattern in a waiting room?

Anxiety often makes breathing faster, shallower, or more effortful. Deliberately slowing the exhale gives your attention a concrete place to go, rather than asking you to reason your way out of every worried thought.

There is some research behind slow breathing and structured breathwork, but it is worth keeping the claims realistic. In a 2023 randomized trial, Balban and colleagues found that five minutes per day of cyclic sighing was associated with improvements in mood and reductions in resting respiratory rate compared with a mindfulness meditation condition. The study did not test people during dental procedures, and it cannot guarantee a quick result for every person (Balban et al., *Cell Reports Medicine*, 2023, DOI: 10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895).

A 2023 meta-analysis by Fincham and colleagues, covering 12 randomized controlled trials and 785 adults, found a small-to-moderate average reduction in self-reported stress from breathwork interventions (standardized effect size g = -0.35). These studies varied in the breathing methods and situations studied. Breathwork is not a cure for anxiety, but it can be a practical coping tool for a difficult few minutes.

Think of this as a reset, not a test. You are not failing if your heart is still pounding or if worried thoughts keep returning. Keep returning to the next slow exhale.

## How can you use it before the appointment begins?

Start with five rounds while seated. Then continue with one round whenever you notice yourself checking the clock, reading alarming possibilities into a sensation, or rehearsing the appointment in your head.

Use the room as a cue:

- When you sit down, do five rounds.
- When your name is called, do one round before standing.
- When you reach the treatment room, do one round before answering questions.
- When you sit in the dental chair or exam room, do one round while your clinician prepares.

This keeps the technique small and repeatable. You do not have to disappear into your phone or close your eyes. You can breathe this way with your eyes open while listening to the receptionist or looking at a fixed point in the room.

If you cannot leave the setting or want an additional discreet grounding approach, see [how to calm down in public when you can't leave](/blog/calm-down-in-public-when-you-cant-leave).

## Can you tell the dentist or doctor that you are anxious?

Yes. Telling staff early can make the appointment more manageable. You do not need to explain your whole history or justify your anxiety. One clear sentence is enough.

You could say:

> “I get quite anxious at medical appointments. It helps me if you tell me what is happening before you begin.”

Or:

> “I am feeling anxious today. Could we go step by step and pause if I raise my hand?”

For dental appointments, it can help to agree on a simple stop signal before treatment begins, such as raising a hand. Ask whether the dentist can briefly check in at natural pauses. You might also ask for a moment to settle into the chair before they start.

Staff cannot always change every part of a procedure, but they can often explain what is next, offer short pauses when clinically appropriate, or adjust the pace of communication. Sharing your concern is useful information, not an inconvenience.

If you are worried about pain, fainting, medication, a past difficult experience, or a particular part of the procedure, say that directly. Your clinician can discuss appropriate options and any safety considerations for your care.

## What should you do once you are in the chair or exam room?

Return to the same breathing pattern, but make it subtler. A full, audible exhale may not feel comfortable in a dental chair, so use a gentle version: inhale through the nose, take a small second inhale if it feels easy, then exhale slowly through the nose or mouth.

Keep your attention narrow. Choose one anchor that does not interfere with care, such as:

- Feeling your heels against the footrest or floor.
- Noticing the weight of your hands resting together.
- Following the sensation of air leaving your nose.

Then pair the anchor with a short phrase: “This is one breath,” or “I only need to do this minute.” The phrase is not meant to prove that everything will feel good. It is a way to stop anxiety from demanding that you solve the entire appointment at once.

If the clinician has agreed to a pause signal, remember that you can use it. Asking for a brief pause to regroup is different from trying to endure until you are overwhelmed.

## What if the anxiety keeps coming back?

Expect it to come in waves. The goal is not to win one round of breathing and never feel anxious again. Each time you notice the surge, do one or two physiological sighs and reconnect with your chosen anchor.

Avoid turning the technique into a repeated check: “Did that work? Am I calm yet?” That question can keep your attention locked on the anxiety. Instead, gently ask, “What is the next small action?” It may be filling out a form, taking a sip of water if permitted, telling the nurse you are anxious, or simply staying seated for another minute.

If appointments are consistently so distressing that you avoid needed dental or medical care, or if fear is disrupting your daily life, support from a licensed mental health professional may help. You can also talk with your dentist or doctor about the pattern. Our guide on [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional) explains when extra support may be useful.

## How can you make the next appointment easier?

Practice this same breathing pattern once when you are not in a waiting room. One minute while sitting on your bed, in your car before work, or while making tea can make it feel more familiar later.

Before the appointment, write one sentence you want to say to staff and put it in your phone notes. For example: “I am anxious about this appointment, and I would appreciate clear explanations and a pause signal.” You can read it aloud or hand over your phone if speaking feels hard.

For an offline prompt when your mind goes blank, you can also open [Nuvin](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/nuvin-anxiety-relief/id6753338724) before you arrive and use a short breathing exercise. The aim is not perfect calm. It is to help you take the next breath, communicate what you need, and get through the next step.

## What is the one thing to remember?

You do not have to feel ready before you begin. In the waiting room, do five gentle physiological sighs. Then tell staff, in one sentence, that you are anxious and what would help. Those are small actions, but they can give you a little more room to cope with a clinical appointment.
