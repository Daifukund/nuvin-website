# Nocturnal Panic Attacks: What to Do When You Wake Up in Panic

Waking up in a panic can feel especially disorienting. The room is dark, you were asleep moments ago, and now your body may feel urgently awake.

For the next minute, do not try to figure out why this is happening or force yourself back to sleep. Give your attention one small, repeatable job: a gentle inhale followed by a slightly longer, unforced exhale.

## What can I do right now when I wake up in a panic?

Stay in the position that feels most steady. You can remain lying down, turn onto your side, or prop yourself up with pillows. There is no need to turn on a bright light or check the time.

Then try this longer-exhale breathing routine:

1. **Let out the breath you are already holding.** Do not push or empty your lungs completely. Just let your exhale soften.
2. **Breathe in gently through your nose.** Aim for a small, quiet inhale, about three seconds if counting helps.
3. **Breathe out slowly and lightly.** Let the exhale last a little longer than the inhale, perhaps five or six seconds. Your jaw, shoulders, and belly do not need to be perfectly relaxed.
4. **Repeat for 10 breaths.** If counting makes you more alert or tense, drop the numbers. Simply think, “in,” then, “longer out.”

The important part is comfort, not precision. Do not take giant breaths, hold your breath, or try to make the sensation disappear on command. A very full breath can feel uncomfortable when you are already scared. Keep the inhale easy and make the out-breath only as long as feels natural.

If your mind says, “Something is wrong,” you do not have to argue with it. Return to the next gentle exhale. Your only goal for these 10 breaths is to make the next minute more manageable.

## Why use a longer exhale for a night panic attack?

During a panic surge, many people start breathing faster, shallower, or more forcefully without meaning to. Slowing the pace gently can give your attention a predictable physical rhythm instead of leaving it fully focused on frightening sensations and thoughts.

There is research supporting slow, structured breathing for stress, although it is not a guaranteed instant treatment for nocturnal panic attacks. A 2023 meta-analysis of 12 randomized controlled trials involving 785 adults found that breathwork interventions were associated with lower self-reported stress than comparison conditions, with a small-to-moderate average effect (Fincham et al., *Scientific Reports*).

A Stanford randomized trial also found that a brief daily cyclic-sighing breathing practice improved mood and reduced resting respiratory rate compared with a mindfulness practice in that study (Balban et al., 2023, *Cell Reports Medicine*, [DOI: 10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)). That research studied regular practice, not people trying to stop a panic attack in sleep in the moment. It supports breathing as a reasonable self-help tool, but it does not mean one pattern works for everyone or replaces clinical care.

For tonight, treat the breathing as an experiment. If a three-in, six-out count feels strained, make it smaller. A comfortable inhale and a slightly longer exhale is enough.

## How do I use this to get back to sleep?

After 10 breaths, check whether you feel even 5 percent less activated. You do not need to feel calm before sleep can return. You only need to stop treating sleep as an emergency task.

Continue the same easy rhythm without counting. Let your exhale be a cue that there is nothing you need to solve right now. If thoughts arrive, such as “I will be exhausted tomorrow” or “What if this happens again?”, notice the thought and come back to the sensation of the air leaving your body.

Try not to check your phone, search symptoms, or repeatedly look at the clock. Those actions can pull your attention back into monitoring and problem-solving. In the dark, your job is smaller: rest your body and keep the next breath gentle.

You may drift off during the exercise, or you may stay awake for a while. Either outcome is okay. Quiet resting is still different from fighting with the panic.

If racing thoughts are keeping you alert after the physical surge has eased, this guide on [quieting racing thoughts at night](/blog) may help you make a simple plan for the next awake moment.

## Why do nocturnal panic attacks happen?

Nocturnal panic attacks are panic episodes that wake someone from sleep. They can feel abrupt because you may not remember a worrying thought or event immediately before waking. The body’s alarm response can be active before your thinking mind has a clear explanation for it.

One episode cannot tell you why it happened. Stress, sleep disruption, anxiety patterns, substances, medications, and sleep or medical conditions can all be worth discussing with a qualified clinician in the right context. Avoid trying to diagnose the cause in the middle of the night. Write down what you remember in the morning instead: when it happened, what you noticed, and anything that seemed different that day or evening.

A short in-the-moment plan can be useful, but recurrent nocturnal panic warrants seeing a professional. A clinician can help assess the pattern and discuss appropriate support. Start with [when to see a professional](/blog) if these episodes keep happening, disrupt your sleep, or make you afraid to go to bed.

## When should I seek urgent help instead of trying to breathe through it?

Use caution with symptoms that are new, severe, different from your usual experience, or that make you think you may be having a medical emergency. Seek urgent medical evaluation rather than assuming it is panic.

The same applies if you are at risk of harming yourself or do not feel able to stay safe. Use local emergency services or immediate crisis support. You deserve help that goes beyond getting through one difficult night.

## What should I do tomorrow after waking up in a panic?

Keep your notes brief and practical. Record the approximate time, what you felt in your body, how long it seemed to last, and whether anything had changed recently, such as sleep, caffeine, alcohol, medication, illness, or stress. This is not to prove a theory. It gives you useful information if you speak with a clinician.

Then practice the same longer-exhale rhythm once during the day, when you are not in a panic. A familiar skill is easier to use at 3 a.m. than a brand-new one. If you want a guided version without opening a bright screen full of information, you can keep a brief exercise ready in the [Nuvin app](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/nuvin-anxiety-relief/id6753338724).

For a broader in-the-moment plan, see [how to stop a panic attack](/blog/how-to-stop-a-panic-attack). For now, return to the smallest next step: one easy inhale, then a longer, softer exhale.
