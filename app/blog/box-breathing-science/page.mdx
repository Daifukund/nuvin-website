# Box Breathing: How to Do 4-4-4-4 Breathing and What Science Says

Box breathing is a simple, paced breathing exercise. You inhale, hold, exhale, and hold again for equal counts, making the four sides of a mental square.

If you feel anxious right now, start here. You do not need to empty your mind or breathe perfectly. Just make your next few breaths slower and gentler.

## How do you do box breathing right now?

Sit or stand in a position that does not restrict your breathing. Let your shoulders drop if that feels comfortable. You can look at a fixed point, close your eyes, or simply soften your gaze.

Use this basic **4-4-4-4 breathing** pattern:

1. **Inhale gently for 4.** Breathe in through your nose if comfortable. Do not try to take the biggest breath possible.
2. **Hold for 4.** Keep your jaw, neck, and shoulders as loose as you can.
3. **Exhale slowly for 4.** Let the air leave without pushing or squeezing it out.
4. **Hold for 4.** Pause comfortably before the next inhale.
5. Repeat for **four rounds**.

You can picture tracing a square: up as you inhale, across as you hold, down as you exhale, and across as you pause.

A count of four is only a starting point. If it feels too long, use a smaller square:

- Inhale for 3
- Hold for 3
- Exhale for 3
- Hold for 3

The point is a smooth, manageable rhythm, not completing a challenge. If holding your breath makes you tense, skip the holds and use a steady inhale and exhale instead.

> **Safety note:** Keep every breath gentle, not forceful. Stop if you feel dizzy, lightheaded, or uncomfortable, and return to your natural breathing.

## What is box breathing, also called square breathing?

Box breathing, sometimes called **square breathing**, is a form of paced breathing with four equal phases. The common version uses four counts for each phase, which is why people call it 4-4-4-4 breathing.

It is not a special kind of lung training, and the number four is not medically magic. It is a memorable structure that can give your attention one simple task during a stressful moment.

Anxiety can pull attention toward racing thoughts, what-ifs, or a strong urge to fix everything immediately. Counting a predictable breathing pattern can provide a small, concrete anchor. The slower pace may also change how your body is breathing compared with quick, shallow, or irregular breaths.

That does not mean box breathing will erase anxiety, solve the situation causing it, or work the same way for everyone. Think of it as a brief regulation skill for an everyday anxious spike, a hard conversation, a crowded commute, or the minutes before a presentation.

## What should you focus on during the four counts?

Try this coaching cue for each side of the box:

**Inhale:** “Easy in.”

Let the breath arrive without lifting your shoulders. If breathing through your nose feels blocked or unpleasant, breathe through your mouth gently instead.

**First hold:** “Nothing to fix.”

A pause can feel unfamiliar when you are keyed up. You are not trying to trap air. It is simply a brief, relaxed pause.

**Exhale:** “Long and soft.”

Let your exhale be quiet and unforced. People sometimes turn breathing exercises into a performance by pulling in too much air or blowing out hard. That can make discomfort more likely. Smaller, easier breaths are usually a better fit for this practice.

**Second hold:** “Wait.”

Notice the support beneath you, such as the chair under your legs or the floor under your feet. Then begin the next round.

If counting itself feels stressful, count only the exhale. Or use your fingers to track the four phases. The method should reduce mental load, not add to it.

## How long should you practice box breathing?

For an anxious moment, begin with **four rounds**, which is often about one to two minutes depending on your pace. Then pause and notice whether you want another few rounds.

A useful range for many people is two to five minutes. More is not automatically better. If you are new to paced breathing, a short practice makes it easier to stay gentle and to learn what rhythm feels comfortable.

You can also practice once when you are relatively calm. That matters because skills are easier to recall under stress when they are familiar. You might do four rounds before opening your inbox, sitting down for a meeting, or getting into bed.

If you are trying box breathing during intense anxiety and the breath holds make you feel more activated, shorten the count or remove the holds. Another option is a pattern with a longer, unheld exhale. For a comparison of approaches, see [4-7-8 breathing, box breathing, and the physiological sigh](/blog/478-vs-box-vs-sigh).

## Does box breathing actually work for anxiety and stress?

It can help some people feel calmer in the moment, but the honest answer is more nuanced than many online claims suggest.

Research on slow, structured breathing as a broad category is promising. Breathing exercises are low-cost, accessible, and may help reduce perceived stress for some adults. Still, studies do not show that one specific breathing pattern works for every person or every kind of anxiety.

A 2023 randomized trial led by researchers at Stanford compared several brief daily practices over 28 days. Participants practiced for about five minutes a day. The groups included structured breathing practices, including box breathing, and a mindfulness meditation comparison. The study found that the structured breathing practices were associated with improved mood and reduced physiological arousal, with **cyclic sighing** showing the strongest results among the practices tested.

That is useful, but it is not proof that box breathing is the best technique for everyone. The study was relatively short, involved a specific group of participants, and tested a daily practice rather than a single emergency-use session. It also compared packages of practice, not every possible way a person might do box breathing at home. You can read about the study in Balban and colleagues, *Cell Reports Medicine* (2023), DOI: [10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895).

If you want the technique that performed best in that particular trial, try our guide to the [physiological sigh](/blog/physiological-sigh-fastest-technique). It is a different pattern, not a replacement you have to use.

## What does the wider breathing research say?

A 2023 meta-analysis by Fincham and colleagues reviewed 12 randomized controlled trials involving 785 adults. Across those studies, breathwork was associated with lower self-reported stress compared with control conditions, with a small-to-moderate average effect size (Hedges’ *g* = -0.35).

This is encouraging evidence for breathwork overall. But it deserves the same careful reading as any health evidence.

The trials used different breathing practices, different comparison groups, and different measures of stress. Many were small, and the study quality and methods varied. The meta-analysis cannot tell us that box breathing specifically is the best pattern, that it will reliably stop panic, or that it should replace professional mental health care when that is needed.

Fincham and colleagues also highlighted the gap between popular enthusiasm for breathwork and the still-developing evidence base. That caution is important. A practice can be worth trying without being a cure, a diagnostic tool, or a guarantee.

The paper is: Fincham GW, et al., “Effect of breathwork on stress and mental health: A meta-analysis of randomised-controlled trials,” *Scientific Reports* (2023).

## Why might a square breathing pattern feel calming?

There are a few plausible reasons, though individual responses vary.

First, a slower, regular rhythm may shift you away from rushed or uneven breathing. Second, counting and following four steps gives your attention a narrow, repeatable task. This can interrupt, for a moment, the cycle of scanning for threats or rehearsing worries.

Third, the exercise creates a deliberate pause before you react. You may not feel completely calm after four rounds, but you might have enough extra space to choose the next small action, such as drinking water, sending one clear message, or stepping outside.

It is important not to turn these possible mechanisms into oversized claims. Researchers are still studying exactly how breathing practices affect stress, mood, attention, and physiological measures, and the answer can depend on the specific technique, person, and setting.

## What if the holds feel uncomfortable?

You do not need to force box breathing. Breath holds are the part most likely to feel awkward for some people, especially when they are already breathing quickly or feeling tense.

Try one of these adjustments while keeping the method simple:

- Make the box smaller, using 3-3-3-3 or even 2-2-2-2.
- Keep the inhale and exhale at four, but make the holds two.
- Skip the holds altogether, inhaling gently for three or four and exhaling gently for three or four.

Changing the count is not failing at box breathing. The best pace is one you can do without straining, gasping, or becoming preoccupied with whether you are doing it correctly.

Stop the exercise if you feel dizzy. Resume normal breathing and choose a non-breath-based grounding activity instead, such as naming five neutral things you can see or feeling both feet press into the floor.

## When is box breathing most useful?

Box breathing tends to fit situations where you need a brief reset and can safely place some attention on your breath. Examples include waiting before an appointment, preparing to speak, transitioning out of work, or taking a pause during an argument before responding.

It may be less useful when the structure feels like another demand, when you cannot comfortably focus on your breath, or when the situation needs a practical response first. For example, if you are late, you may need to send the message and make a plan. Four gentle rounds can still be a pause, but they do not remove the real-world task.

You can use this exercise privately. Nobody needs to know you are counting. If it helps, trace the square with a finger against your leg or silently count “one, two, three, four.”

## How can you make box breathing easier to remember?

Use one short instruction: **in, hold, out, hold.**

Practice it at a predictable cue for a week, such as before lunch or after you lock your computer. The goal is not to build a perfect streak. It is to make the pattern familiar enough that it comes to mind when you need it.

## When should you get more support than a breathing exercise?

Breathing skills can be part of caring for everyday stress, but they are not a substitute for assessment or treatment when anxiety is persistent, worsening, or interfering with your life. Consider talking with a qualified health or mental health professional if anxiety is making it hard to work, sleep, connect with people, or do things you need or want to do.

## What is the bottom line on box breathing?

Box breathing is a straightforward paced-breathing exercise: inhale, hold, exhale, hold, using equal counts. It has a reasonable evidence-informed place as a self-help tool for everyday stress and anxious moments, but it is not a cure-all and the research does not establish it as uniquely superior.

Try four easy rounds. Keep the breath light. Adjust or skip the holds if they are uncomfortable. Stop if you feel dizzy. The most useful technique is usually the one you can do gently and return to when you need a small pause.
