# Fear of Having Another Panic Attack: How to Break the Panic-About-Panic Cycle

If you have had a panic attack before, the fear of having another one can become its own source of anxiety. You may scan your body, cancel plans, stay close to home, or feel alarmed by the smallest sign of nervousness.

This is often called **panic about panic**. The goal is not to force yourself to feel calm immediately. It is to practice a different response when the fear shows up.

## Can you practice responding to panic fear right now?

Try this acceptance-based practice the next time you notice, "What if I panic?" It can take a minute or two. You do not need to feel convinced by every word. Just practice the steps.

### 1. Name what is happening

Quietly say to yourself:

> "This is fear of panic. My mind is predicting danger because it remembers a hard experience."

Be specific. Rather than, "Something is wrong," try, "I am noticing a rush of anticipatory anxiety." Naming the pattern creates a little space between you and the prediction.

You are not arguing with the thought or trying to prove it false. You are recognizing it as a thought, not an instruction.

### 2. Make room for the uncomfortable feeling

Try this phrase:

> "I do not like this feeling, but I can let it be here for this moment."

Notice where the fear lands in your body. Perhaps it is tightness in your chest, heat in your face, a fluttering stomach, or restless energy. See whether you can soften around it by 5 percent, rather than trying to make it disappear.

This is not about liking panic or giving up. It is about dropping the exhausting fight with the possibility of panic. Fighting every sensation can teach your brain that sensations themselves are emergencies. Allowing a sensation to be present, while staying grounded in what you are doing, offers your brain a new experience.

If it helps, put both feet on the floor and press them down gently. Look around and name three ordinary things you can see. Keep your attention partly on the room, not entirely on monitoring your body.

### 3. Answer the demand for certainty

Fear of another panic attack often asks impossible questions: "What if it happens here?" "What if I cannot cope?" "What if it never stops?"

Instead of trying to achieve total certainty, answer with a willingness statement:

> "Maybe I will feel anxious. If I do, I can take this one moment at a time."

Or:

> "I can carry some uncertainty and still continue with the next small thing."

This is a key shift. The aim is not, "I must guarantee that panic will not happen." The aim is, "I can choose my next step even while my mind is making scary predictions."

For more support with releasing the pressure to calm down right away, read [you don't have to calm down instantly](/blog/you-dont-have-to-calm-down-instantly).

### 4. Return to one small, chosen action

Ask: **What would I be doing for the next two minutes if I were not trying to prevent panic?**

Choose an action that is small and concrete:

- Stay in the meeting for two more minutes.
- Walk to the end of the block and back.
- Take one item to the checkout.
- Send the text you have been delaying.
- Sit at the restaurant table and take one sip of water.

The action should be challenging enough to matter, but not so large that it feels like a test you must pass. You are practicing return, not proving bravery.

As you do it, repeat: "Anxiety can come along. I am still doing this next step."

### 5. Stop checking for a verdict

Afterward, your mind may ask, "Did it work? Am I calm enough now?" That checking can pull you back into body-scanning.

Try responding: "I practiced responding differently. That is enough for now."

Then return your attention to what is in front of you. The measure of practice is not whether you achieved perfect calm. It is whether you made a little room for discomfort and rejoined your life.

## Why does the fear of another panic attack become so strong?

A panic attack can be intensely frightening. Afterward, it makes sense that your mind wants to prevent a repeat. It may start watching for early signs, such as a faster heartbeat, dizziness, tension, or a rush of adrenaline.

The problem is that close monitoring can make ordinary shifts in sensation feel important and threatening. A sensation triggers a thought such as, "Here it comes." That thought raises fear. The added fear can intensify the sensations, which seems to confirm the prediction.

That is the panic-about-panic loop:

1. You notice a sensation or anxious thought.
2. You interpret it as a sign that panic may be coming.
3. Fear and monitoring increase.
4. The sensations feel stronger or more alarming.
5. You escape, avoid, seek reassurance, or keep checking.
6. You get short-term relief, but your brain learns that the situation or sensation needed escaping.

This does not mean you are causing your anxiety or doing anything wrong. It means your protective system may be trying too hard to keep you safe.

## What is anticipatory anxiety panic?

**Anticipatory anxiety** is anxiety about a future event or experience. In this case, it is the fear that another panic attack could happen, often before a place, activity, trip, meeting, or social situation.

It can feel especially frustrating because it may arrive when you are not having a full panic attack. You might spend hours worrying about whether one will happen, planning exits, or avoiding things that matter to you.

Anticipatory anxiety is often maintained by the search for certainty. Your mind may believe that if it thinks hard enough, checks carefully enough, or avoids enough situations, it can eliminate all risk. But complete certainty is not available, and the search can keep the alarm system switched on.

The response-and-return practice above is designed for this moment. It helps you meet the prediction without organizing your whole day around it.

## Does avoidance keep the cycle going?

Avoidance often brings immediate relief. If you leave a crowded store and your fear drops, your brain may conclude, "Leaving saved me." Next time, it may send an even stronger urge to avoid the store.

Over time, the list of feared situations can grow. You may start avoiding driving, exercise, queues, public places, being alone, travel, or any situation where leaving feels difficult. Safety behaviors can work similarly, including always sitting by an exit, carrying reassurance items you feel unable to be without, or repeatedly asking someone to confirm that you are okay.

Avoidance is understandable. It is not a personal failure. But if you want more freedom, the longer-term direction is usually gradual return, in manageable steps, while practicing a new relationship with anxious thoughts and sensations.

Do not turn this into a harsh command to push through everything. Start with one small situation you have been avoiding and make the step realistic. Repeating small returns matters more than taking one huge leap.

## What does acceptance mean, and what does it not mean?

Acceptance means acknowledging that anxiety is here without spending all your energy trying to control, suppress, or escape it. It is an approach used in cognitive behavioral therapy, including acceptance- and commitment-based work. These approaches focus on changing unhelpful patterns of responding and helping people take actions that fit their values.

Acceptance does **not** mean that you want panic, that you must endure overwhelming situations alone, or that you should ignore problems in your life. It means you can say, "This is uncomfortable, and I can still decide what matters for the next moment."

Some self-help approaches, including the popular DARE-style approach, use a similar invitation to meet anxious feelings with less resistance. The evidence-aligned part is the broader use of cognitive behavioral and acceptance-based principles. No single phrase or method works instantly for everyone.

## When should you get additional support?

If fear of panic is restricting your daily life, it may be a sign of panic disorder, and it is important to speak with a qualified health professional. A clinician can assess what is going on and discuss support options, including therapy approaches designed for panic and avoidance.

Consider reaching out sooner if you are avoiding important responsibilities, becoming increasingly isolated, relying heavily on substances to cope, or feeling unable to manage alone. You can learn more in [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional).

If you feel at risk of harming yourself or unable to stay safe, seek urgent local emergency or crisis support now.

## What can you remember the next time fear shows up?

You do not have to win an argument with anxiety. You do not have to promise yourself that panic will never happen again.

Try this instead:

> "This is fear of panic. I can make room for this feeling, and I can take one small step toward my life."

Practice that response when the fear is mild, too. Repetition helps make it more available during harder moments.
