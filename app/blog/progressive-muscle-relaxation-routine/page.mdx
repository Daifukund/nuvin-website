# Progressive Muscle Relaxation: A 5-Minute Tension-Release Routine

If anxiety has your shoulders up, jaw clenched, or stomach tight, try this progressive muscle relaxation routine now. You do not need equipment, an internet connection, or a perfectly quiet room.

Progressive muscle relaxation, often called PMR, works by briefly tightening a muscle group, then letting it soften. The contrast can make tension easier to notice and release.

Set a timer for five minutes if that helps. Sit supported or lie down somewhere comfortable. Let your hands rest loosely. You can close your eyes if that feels comfortable, or simply lower your gaze.

## How do you do this 5-minute progressive muscle relaxation routine?

Before you start, use only gentle, comfortable effort, about 30 to 50 percent of your maximum squeeze. PMR is not a strength exercise. Do not push through pain, cramping, or a joint that does not feel right.

For each area below:

1. Breathe in as you gently tense the muscles for about 5 seconds.
2. Breathe out and fully release for 10 to 15 seconds.
3. Notice the difference between bracing and softening, without trying to force a particular feeling.

If counting makes you more tense, skip the count. Simply tense briefly, then give yourself a longer pause to let go.

### 1. Start with your hands and arms

Make soft fists. Press your fingers into your palms and gently bend your wrists or draw your forearms in toward your body.

Hold for a few seconds. Notice the effort in your fingers, palms, and forearms.

Now release. Let your fingers uncurl. Rest your hands heavily in your lap, by your sides, or on the bed. Notice whether there is warmth, pulsing, heaviness, or simply less effort.

### 2. Soften your shoulders and upper back

Lift your shoulders slightly toward your ears. You might also gently draw your shoulder blades back, as if you were making a little space across the front of your chest.

Hold, then release on an exhale. Let your shoulders drop only as far as they naturally want to. There is no need to pull them down.

Pause here. Anxiety often shows up as an ongoing, barely noticeable shoulder brace. See if you can recognize the moment when you stop doing that work.

### 3. Unclench your face and jaw

Gently press your lips together and lightly tighten your jaw. You can also squeeze your eyes shut if that is comfortable for you.

Then release. Let your teeth separate. Let your tongue rest broadly in the bottom of your mouth. Soften the space around your eyes and forehead.

Keep this part especially light. If you have jaw pain or a jaw condition, skip jaw tightening and simply practice letting the jaw hang loose for a few breaths.

### 4. Release your stomach and lower back

Gently tighten your abdominal muscles, as though preparing for a light tap on the stomach. Do not hold your breath.

Release on the exhale. Let your belly move naturally with the next breath. If you notice yourself trying to make your stomach perfectly relaxed, return to one simple task: stop tightening it.

You can add a small, comfortable arch through the lower back while tensing, then return to neutral as you release. Skip this if it aggravates back pain.

### 5. Let your legs and feet settle

Press your feet gently into the floor, or point your toes away from you if lying down. Tighten your thighs and calves without locking your knees.

Hold briefly. Then release all the way down through your legs. Let your feet feel supported by the floor, mattress, or chair.

Finish by taking one unforced breath. On the exhale, scan from forehead to feet and ask: “Where could I do five percent less holding?” You do not need to eliminate every sensation. A small reduction in bracing is enough.

## What should you notice while doing PMR?

The point is not to make anxiety disappear on command. The practice is to give your nervous system clear physical information: this is what tightening feels like, and this is what easing off feels like.

You may notice a sense of heaviness, warmth, tingling, or calm after releasing a muscle group. You may also notice very little at first. Both experiences are okay. PMR is a skill of attention as much as it is a tension-release routine.

If your mind races during the exercise, do not argue with it. Gently return to the body part you are working on. Try using simple labels: “tense,” “release,” or “soften.”

If five minutes feels like too much right now, do just hands, shoulders, and jaw. A shorter routine you can actually use is more helpful than an ideal routine you avoid.

## Does progressive muscle relaxation help anxiety?

Research suggests that relaxation training, including progressive muscle relaxation, can help reduce anxiety symptoms for some people. A 2008 meta-analysis by Manzoni and colleagues in *BMC Psychiatry* reviewed 27 studies and found a moderate overall effect for relaxation training on anxiety, reported as Cohen's d of about 0.57.

That is encouraging evidence, but it does not mean PMR works instantly or equally for everyone. The studies varied in the people involved and the kinds of relaxation training used. PMR is best viewed as a practical self-help skill, not a replacement for individualized mental health care when you need it.

Many people find it more useful with repetition. Practicing once a day when you are relatively calm can make the steps easier to remember when tension rises during a stressful moment.

## How can you make the routine easier when anxiety is high?

Keep the effort gentle. When anxiety is intense, hard muscle squeezing can feel like more activation. Use a light tense-and-release instead.

You can also simplify the routine to one area. For example, tense both hands for a few seconds, release, then lift and release your shoulders. Repeat that sequence twice. This gives you a physical reset without needing to think through a longer script.

Try pairing PMR with a predictable moment in your day, such as after closing your laptop, before getting into bed, or when you arrive home. For a very brief transition practice, try this [60-second reset between tasks](/blog/60-second-reset-between-tasks).

Some people prefer more active movement when sitting still feels uncomfortable. In that case, a gentle [shaking and stretching tension-release practice](/blog/shaking-stretching-release-tension) may be a better fit.

## When should you skip or adapt progressive muscle relaxation?

Do not tense an area that is injured, painful, recently operated on, or prone to cramping. Adapt around it, or skip it. For example, you can relax your hands and shoulders without involving your legs.

If you have a health condition that affects movement, muscles, joints, or circulation, or you are unsure whether tensing a particular area is appropriate, ask a qualified clinician for guidance.

PMR can be one useful tool for everyday anxiety, but ongoing anxiety that is interfering with sleep, work, relationships, or daily life deserves more support. Read [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional) for signs that it may be time to reach out.

## What is the simplest way to finish the practice?

Do not rush straight back into the stressful task. Take ten seconds to notice your contact points: feet on the floor, back against the chair, or body against the bed.

Then choose one next action that is small and concrete. Drink water. Send one message. Stand up. Return to the first line of the task. PMR does not have to solve the whole day. It can give you a steadier starting point for the next minute.

## What evidence supports this technique?

Manzoni GM, Pagnini F, Castelnuovo G, Molinari E. “Relaxation training for anxiety: a ten-years systematic review with meta-analysis.” *BMC Psychiatry*. 2008;8:41. The review included 27 studies and reported a moderate overall effect of relaxation training for anxiety. As with any meta-analysis, the findings reflect varied studies and do not guarantee an individual result.
