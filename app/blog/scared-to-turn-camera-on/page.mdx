# Camera Anxiety on Video Calls: A Fast Calm-Down Routine

If you are staring at the “Turn camera on” button with a tight chest, hot face, or a strong urge to disappear, do this before you join: use a **cyclic sighing** breathing reset for about one minute.

You do not need to feel perfectly confident before switching on your camera. The immediate goal is smaller: give your body a little more room to settle so you can join the call and take the next useful step.

## What should I do in the minute before I turn my camera on?

Try this seated, with both feet on the floor if that is comfortable. Keep your shoulders loose. You can look at one steady point in the room rather than at your screen.

1. **Breathe in gently through your nose.** Do not take your biggest possible breath.
2. **Take a second, short inhale through your nose** to top up the breath.
3. **Exhale slowly through your mouth** until your lungs feel comfortably empty. Let the exhale be longer than both inhales together.
4. **Pause briefly**, then repeat.

Do 3 to 5 rounds. For many people, that takes around 45 to 90 seconds.

The important part is not perfect timing. It is the long, unforced exhale. If counting helps, you might make the first inhale about 2 seconds, the second about 1 second, and the exhale about 5 to 6 seconds. If counting makes you more tense, skip it.

Then make the task very small: click **Join**, keep your camera off for the first few seconds if the meeting allows it, and turn it on once you are oriented. Or turn it on right away, then look at the speaker or your notes instead of scanning your own image.

If you feel lightheaded, tingly, or more uncomfortable while breathing, return to your normal breathing. Slower is not always better, and forcing deep breaths can make some people feel worse.

## Why can this help when video-call anxiety hits?

Camera anxiety video call moments can feel oddly high-stakes. You may be managing the meeting topic, other people’s expressions, the awareness that you are visible, and a little square showing your own face. Your body can interpret all that social attention as a threat, even when the call itself is routine.

A longer, gentle exhale is a simple way to shift attention toward a physical task when your mind is racing. It may also help reduce the sense of breathing too fast or too shallow that can accompany stress. It is not a switch that removes anxiety on command. Think of it as creating a small pause between the alarm feeling and what you do next.

There is some encouraging research on structured breathing, with important limits. In a 2023 randomized trial, Balban and colleagues found that a daily five-minute cyclic sighing practice was associated with improved mood and reduced respiratory rate compared with some comparison practices over 28 days. That study tested a regular practice, not a guaranteed one-minute fix before a Zoom meeting. [Balban et al., *Cell Reports Medicine*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)

A 2023 meta-analysis by Fincham and colleagues, covering 12 randomized controlled trials with 785 adults, found breathwork interventions were associated with lower self-reported stress than control conditions (standardized effect size g = -0.35). The studies varied in their methods and quality, so breathwork should not be presented as a cure-all. Still, a brief breathing practice is a low-effort option many people can try for everyday stress. [Fincham et al., *Scientific Reports*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y)

## How do I use the reset once the meeting has started?

You do not need to visibly breathe in a special way, and you do not need to close your eyes. Use one quiet round at a natural pause.

For example, when someone else is speaking:

- Put both feet down and notice the pressure of the floor.
- Take the gentle inhale, small top-up inhale, and slow exhale.
- On the exhale, let your jaw unclench and drop your shoulders a fraction.
- Bring your attention back to one concrete meeting task: the current speaker’s point, the document, or the next sentence you want to say.

If you are about to speak, try one round before you unmute. Then begin with a prepared first sentence, such as, “I have one update on that,” or “My question is about the timeline.” Starting is often the hardest part. You may find our guide to [speaking up in meetings when you feel anxious](/blog/speaking-up-in-meetings-anxiety) useful for that specific moment.

Do not keep checking whether the breathing has “worked.” That turns the exercise into another performance test. Instead, ask: “Can I stay for the next 30 seconds?” Then focus on the call.

## What if I am scared to turn my camera on because I can see myself?

If seeing your own tile makes your anxiety spike, use the platform’s **hide self-view** option after confirming your camera framing. This is different from turning off your camera. Other people can still see you, but you do not have to monitor your own face throughout the meeting.

Before the call, take 20 seconds to make the visual setup less demanding:

- Place the camera roughly at eye level if you can.
- Use a simple light source in front of you, rather than worrying about looking polished.
- Close unrelated tabs and notifications.
- Put one short note near your screen: “Listen. Breathe out. One point at a time.”

These are not rules for looking better on camera. They reduce extra decisions when you already feel activated.

It can also help to name the fear accurately. Perhaps it is, “They will notice I look nervous,” “I will be called on,” or “I will not know where to look.” Naming the concern does not prove it will happen. It gives you a specific problem to work with while you breathe.

## Should I wait until I feel calm before I join?

Usually, no. Waiting for all anxiety to vanish can make the camera button feel even more powerful.

Try a more workable threshold: after 3 to 5 breathing rounds, join while you still feel some nerves. Your job is not to demonstrate calmness. Your job is to participate as well as you can while making room for discomfort.

For a high-pressure call, rehearse the sequence once earlier in the day: open the meeting link, set your frame, hide self-view, do three cyclic sighs, and say your first sentence out loud. A small rehearsal can make the live moment more familiar. If the call is a presentation, see [how to calm down before a presentation](/blog/calm-down-before-a-presentation).

If you use an iPhone and want a short prompt without searching online right before a meeting, you can also open [Nuvin](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/nuvin-anxiety-relief/id6753338724) for an offline breathing or grounding exercise. It is a self-help tool for everyday anxiety, not a substitute for medical or mental health care.

## When is camera anxiety worth getting more support for?

Many people dislike video calls, especially when they are new, evaluative, or frequent. But it is worth talking with a qualified mental health professional if fear of being seen is persistently disrupting your work, school, relationships, or ability to attend necessary appointments. Support can help you understand the pattern and build a plan that fits your circumstances.

You can read more about [when to see a professional for anxiety](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional). If your anxiety comes with new, severe, or concerning physical symptoms, seek medical advice to rule out physical causes.
