# Reassurance Seeking Anxiety: Why Checking Feeds the Cycle

When anxiety spikes, checking can feel like the most sensible thing to do. You may search for an answer, ask someone if everything is okay, reread a message, check your body, review a decision, or return to the same question one more time.

Try this first: **do not answer the anxious question for the next 10 minutes.** Instead, practice the uncertainty pause below.

This is not about proving that your fear is untrue. It is about noticing when checking has become the thing that keeps the fear active.

## What is the 10-minute uncertainty pause?

The uncertainty pause is one small practice for interrupting reassurance-seeking anxiety. You delay the check, name what is happening, and make room for not knowing without trying to settle the question immediately.

Use it with a recurring urge to seek reassurance or check, such as repeatedly asking a loved one for certainty, rereading a work message, looking up symptoms, reviewing a memory, or checking a lock again after you have already checked it.

Do not use this exercise to decide whether a health concern needs medical care. If you have a physical concern that needs assessment, contact an appropriate healthcare professional. Repeated online searching or asking others for reassurance is not a substitute for an evaluation.

### Step 1: Catch the exact urge

Pause at the point of action. Put the phone down, move your hand away from the lock, or stop composing the text asking someone to reassure you.

Then complete this sentence:

> “I have the urge to check or ask because I want certainty about ______.”

Be specific. For example:

- “I want certainty that I did not make a mistake.”
- “I want certainty that they are not upset with me.”
- “I want certainty that I will not regret this decision.”

This matters because anxiety can disguise the urge as a problem-solving emergency. Naming the urge separates the feeling of urgency from the action of checking.

### Step 2: Name the short-term payoff

Say, silently or out loud:

> “Checking might give me a brief drop in anxiety. It will not give me lasting certainty.”

This is not a criticism. Reassurance-seeking works in the short term, which is why the habit is so compelling. A reply from a friend, a search result, or one more review of the situation may bring a moment of relief.

The difficulty is what happens next. Your brain can learn, “I felt anxious, I checked, then the feeling changed. Checking must be necessary.” The next anxious thought then arrives with a stronger urge to check again.

### Step 3: Choose a clear delay

Set a timer for 10 minutes. If 10 minutes feels out of reach, start with two minutes. The goal is not to force the anxiety away during that time. The goal is to practice not performing the reassurance ritual right away.

Make the delay concrete:

> “For the next 10 minutes, I will not search, ask, reread, inspect, review, or seek a guarantee about this.”

Try not to replace one form of checking with another. For instance, if you are resisting asking a friend whether they are angry, repeatedly scanning their last message for clues is still checking.

### Step 4: Answer uncertainty, not the fear

Anxiety often asks for a verdict: “Is this definitely fine?” “What if I missed something?” “Can someone promise me this will work out?”

Instead of debating the content, offer one response:

> “Maybe, maybe not. I can carry uncertainty for 10 minutes.”

You do not need to believe a positive statement. You do not need to persuade yourself that nothing bad will happen. The practice is allowing the unanswered question to be present without treating it as an instruction.

This acceptance-oriented response is consistent with CBT and ACT approaches. It also resembles the acceptance stance used in the DARE approach: acknowledge the anxious experience and reduce the struggle to get rid of it immediately. It is not a guarantee about the outcome you fear.

### Step 5: Return to one chosen action

For the remainder of the timer, do one activity that uses your attention or body. Keep it simple and nearby: wash a mug, fold laundry, walk to the end of the street, continue one work task, or notice five objects in the room.

The purpose is not distraction as a way to erase anxiety. It is evidence to yourself that an anxious urge can be present while you do something else.

If your mind says, “Just check once,” return to the same line:

> “I notice the urge. I am practicing a delay.”

You may need to repeat that line many times. Repetition does not mean you are doing the exercise incorrectly.

### Step 6: Review the process, not the outcome

When the timer ends, ask three questions:

1. What did I want certainty about?
2. What checking action did I delay?
3. What was it like to let the question remain open?

Do not grade the exercise by whether anxiety disappeared. A more useful measure is whether you created even a small gap between the urge and the check.

If you choose to check after the timer, notice that choice without turning it into a failure. Then try another delay next time. Changing a well-rehearsed cycle is usually gradual.

## Why does reassurance seeking make anxiety worse over time?

Reassurance seeking can become a loop:

1. An intrusive thought, doubt, sensation, or uncertainty appears.
2. Anxiety rises.
3. You check, research, ask, review, or seek a promise.
4. Anxiety shifts temporarily.
5. Your mind learns that certainty-seeking was the response to anxiety.
6. The next doubt feels more important and more urgent.

This is often called **negative reinforcement**. The behavior is strengthened because it briefly removes or reduces discomfort.

Cognitive-behavioral models of anxiety and obsessive-compulsive problems have long described this pattern. Paul Salkovskis' cognitive-behavioral account emphasized how interpretations of intrusive thoughts and neutralizing behaviors, including checking and reassurance, can maintain distress rather than resolve it (Salkovskis, 1985).

That model does not mean every question, medical appointment, or request for advice is reassurance-seeking. People need information, support, and professional assessment at times. The key difference is the function of the action.

A useful question is:

> “Am I gathering new, practical information once, or am I trying to get rid of uncertainty until I feel completely certain?”

Complete certainty is a moving target. When the real goal becomes “feel certain right now,” no answer tends to hold for long.

## What counts as a checking behavior?

A checking behavior is an action you repeat to reduce anxiety or settle a doubt. It can be visible, mental, digital, or social.

Examples include rereading messages for hidden meaning, repeatedly checking that an appliance is off, reviewing a conversation in your mind, comparing sensations online, asking several people the same question, or returning to a decision after you have already made it.

Some checking is practical. Checking an address once before leaving is different from reopening the map repeatedly because uncertainty keeps returning. The difference is not always obvious, so focus on the pattern:

- Is the same question returning after you have already answered it?
- Does relief fade quickly?
- Do you feel compelled to repeat the behavior?
- Is checking taking time away from sleep, work, relationships, or daily life?

If the pattern is persistent or disruptive, a licensed mental health professional can help you assess it and build a tailored plan. You can also read more about [when to see a professional](/blog/when-to-see-a-professional).

## What should you expect when you stop checking right away?

At first, delaying reassurance can make the urge feel louder. That does not tell you what the feared outcome means. It tells you that you are interrupting a familiar response pattern.

Avoid turning the uncertainty pause into another way to monitor yourself. You do not need to track whether your heart rate changed, whether the thought went away, or whether you did the practice perfectly. Notice the urge, delay the ritual, and return to the next chosen action.

The broader skill is willingness: “I may not get certainty in this moment, and I can choose what I do next.” For more practice with this skill, see [how to sit with uncertainty](/blog/sit-with-uncertainty).

Health-related reassurance loops deserve particular care. If anxiety leads you to repeatedly search symptoms, [health anxiety and Googling symptoms](/blog/health-anxiety-googling-symptoms) explains why online checking can become self-perpetuating. For a health concern, seek appropriate clinical advice rather than relying on repeated searches or repeated reassurance from people around you.

## When is it time to get professional support?

Consider contacting a licensed mental health professional if reassurance seeking or checking feels hard to control, consumes substantial time, affects work or relationships, disrupts sleep, or makes your world smaller.

CBT, including approaches that address compulsions and reassurance behaviors, and acceptance-based approaches can offer structured support. A professional can help distinguish reasonable problem-solving from anxiety-maintaining rituals and can adapt practice to your circumstances.

For a small in-the-moment prompt, you might save this sentence in your notes app: “I am noticing the urge for certainty. I can delay the check and take one next step.” Nuvin offers offline grounding and breathing exercises if you want a brief way to return attention to the present while the timer runs.

## What is the main point to remember?

Reassurance is not harmful because it is weak or because you should handle everything alone. It becomes a trap when it is repeatedly used to eliminate uncertainty and the relief does not last.

Your next practice does not need to be dramatic. Pick one recurring check. Delay it briefly. Let the question be unanswered for that period. Then return to something you value doing.
